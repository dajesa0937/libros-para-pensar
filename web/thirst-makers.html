PROLOGUE

The First Liar

Nobody remembers his name, and that was his first victory.

He sold oil in the lower market of Ur, on the corner where the wall held shade until noon. It was not a good stall. The oil was not good either: second-pressed, cloudy at the bottom of the jar, exactly like the oil the other nine men on the same street were selling. For eleven years he shouted what they shouted — oil, good oil, fresh oil — and for eleven years he earned what they earned, which was almost nothing.

Until the morning his voice gave out.

He had been three days with a ruined throat. He could not shout. So when a woman stopped in front of his jars, he had to do something he had never once done in that market: speak quietly. And for her to come close enough to hear him, he had to say something worth coming close for.

He said:

"This oil comes from a grove behind the temple."

It was not true. It came from where all the oil on that street came from: the southern terraces, where the trees grow crooked and the olives taste of dust. But the woman went very still, and he saw something in her face he had not seen in eleven years of shouting: he saw that she wanted it to be true.

She paid three times the price. She did not haggle. She carried the jar away pressed against her chest the way you carry a child.

He watched her go and felt two things at once, both with the same force. The first was an animal gladness, hot, almost obscene. The second had no name yet. It would take centuries to get one.

That afternoon he sold all eleven jars. He went home with more silver than he had ever held and could not sleep. Not from guilt: from arithmetic. He spent the night working out what else he could say.

The next day, though, something happened that was not in his plans.

The woman came back. She was carrying the empty jar and a different face.

"My mother is dying," she said. "I rubbed the oil into her hands and her forehead. I told her where it came from. She slept peacefully for the first time in a month."

He looked at her. The lie was ready in his mouth, the same one as yesterday, improved even: he could add the priest's name, the age of the tree, the colour of the soil. He understood, with a precision that frightened him, that he could keep this up for years.

And instead he said:

"The oil is from the southern terraces. Like everyone's."

The silence went on a long time. He waited for the shouting, the scene, the hand at his throat. The woman lowered the jar slowly. Then she said:

"Fill it."

She came back every month for nineteen years. She brought her sisters, her neighbours, the wife of a scribe who in turn brought the wife of a judge. When he died, the stall on the shaded corner was the most expensive in the lower market, and not one of his sons could explain why, because the oil was still the same cloudy oil from the southern terraces.

So in two days he learned the only two things there are to learn about this business, and humanity has not added one since:

That a lie is fast.

And that the truth lasts.

Almost everything we have built since, we built by choosing speed.

About thirty years after that, in Babylon, a king had it carved into a slab of black stone eight feet high: what should happen to anyone who falsified a measure. The stone is still standing. You can go and see it; it is in Paris, behind glass, and people photograph themselves in front of it without reading it.

That they had to carve it in stone says something.

That they carved it so early says more.

CHAPTER 1

The Missing Word

Two thousand people are waiting for a man to sell them a glass of tap water.

Tomás Alcázar lets them wait. He learned long ago that silence is the one part of the job you cannot outsource. Ten seconds of empty stage is worth more than any slide, and he is at twenty-two, counted, with the glass in his hand and his eyes on a point at the back of the hall where there is nobody.

"What is this worth?" he says at last.

Someone in the third row shouts: "Nothing!" Laughter. Good. There is always one.

"Nothing," Tomás repeats. "Correct. I filled it in the men's toilet eleven minutes ago. There are cameras, if anyone would like to check."

He sets the glass on the lectern. He puts his hands in his pockets.

"I am going to try to sell it for two hundred euros. If I succeed, none of you should ever trust me again. And yet, tomorrow, a good many of you are going to hire me."

Another laugh, shorter. They are not comfortable now. Perfect.

"On the fourth of August, 1943," he begins, "in a village in Sicily whose name does not matter, a woman called Rosa picked up her nine-month-old son and walked eleven miles in the sun—"

It is not true. There is no Rosa, no Sicily, no child. He invented her six years ago on a train and he has told it a hundred and forty times, and every time he tells it slightly better, because every time he knows with more precision exactly where the room is breathing. He talks about the heat. The dust. The moment Rosa finds a fountain and discovers it is dry. The second in which she understands that the problem is no longer water, it is time. A stranger's canteen.

When he finishes — four and a half minutes; he has timed it — he lifts the glass again.

"Two hundred euros," he says. "Who?"

Fourteen hands go up.

They give him a standing ovation. They always give him a standing ovation. Tomás smiles, presses his palms together in an apologetic gesture he has rehearsed, says the four closing lines that leave a room feeling it has learned something grave and beautiful, and walks down the side steps with the glass still in his hand.

The corridor behind the stage has grey carpet and a trolley of real water bottles. Three people with lanyards are waiting for him. Tomás raises one finger, says "one minute," walks into the men's toilet — the same one, twenty minutes on — locks himself in the last cubicle and throws up.

It has been happening since March. Not always. Only when he does it very well.

He is fifty-one years old and he has one sentence.

It is in three university textbooks, in four languages, and in a tattoo an intern once showed him on her forearm, which was the worst professional moment of his life and also, if he is honest, one of the best:

Don't sell the water. Sell the thirst.

He wrote it at twenty-nine, in the margin of a napkin, arguing with Elena at three in the morning. He did not intend it to be anything. It was a way of winning an argument.

What he meant then was almost innocent: that nobody cares about your product, that they care about their life, and that your job is to find the point where one touches the other. That is what he used to teach. That is what he still says on stages.

But the sentence slipped its lead. Today it is used to explain why an app has to interrupt you every forty minutes, why a cream has to make you notice a line you had not seen, why a teenager in Manila and another in Bilbao study the same part of their own face at the same hour of the night.

Manufacture the thirst. It turned out you could industrialise it.

Tomás has never said in public that he regrets it, partly because he is not sure it is true. What there is, is smaller and more uncomfortable than regret. It is this: for about twenty years he has not said anything he entirely believes, and he has discovered that it does not show. Not from outside, and not, on most days, from inside either.

The man is waiting for him in the hotel lobby, standing, hands crossed in front of him like a well-mannered nightclub doorman.

"Sabino Ruiz," he says. "Quiral."

Tomás knows the name of the company. Everyone knows the name of the company without quite knowing what it does, which is precisely what a company does once it can afford to.

"The Rosa thing was good," says Sabino.

"Thank you."

"She doesn't exist, does she?"

Tomás looks at him properly for the first time. Sabino is perhaps sixty, in a suit with no visible label, with the face of a man who has slept well every night of his life.

"No," says Tomás.

"Right." Sabino nods, without judgement, like a man confirming a train time. "That was the part I liked best. Do you have twenty minutes?"

It is forty. At a table at the back, over two coffees going cold, Sabino lays out the commission with an administrative calm that makes it all sound smaller than it is.

Quiral has built an engine. He does not call it artificial intelligence — he says the phrase has stopped meaning anything — and he has no interest in discussing the technology. What interests him is the material.

"We've given it everything we could buy," he says. "Every advertisement archived since 1890. The speeches. The sales letters. The sermons, which are the most useful part, incidentally. The treatises on rhetoric. The scripts. The wartime pamphlets from both sides of every war. And it doesn't work."

"Why not?"

"Because it writes like everybody." Sabino shrugs. "Which is what we taught it. We gave it the average of human persuasion and it hands us back the average of human persuasion. It is impeccable. It is unbearable."

"And what do you want from me?"

"For you to teach it the difference." Sabino stirs the coffee he has not drunk. "I need someone to go through that archive and mark, one by one, where the trick is. Not what was said: why it worked. And the opposite, too. Where somebody told the truth and it worked anyway, or better. Nobody has ever done that, because nobody knows how. You have been doing it from memory for thirty years and charging for it."

Tomás looks at the cup. He notices, with unpleasant precision, the whole mechanism: the flattery, the scale, the word nobody, the pause. It is good work. It is his own work, performed on him.

"And when it's trained?" he asks.

"It launches on the fourteenth of November."

"And what does it say, on the fourteenth of November?"

For the first time, Sabino smiles properly.

"That's the other thing I want to buy. The first sentence the machine says to the world, you are going to write. By hand. And nobody will ever know it was you."

Tomás takes a moment to answer. Then he says yes, and does not trouble to pretend to himself that he is doing it out of curiosity.

He is doing it because it has been three years since anyone gave him anything he could not solve in his sleep.

He gets home at twenty past eleven.

The flat is clean in the way that houses are clean when nothing happens in them. There are a hundred and forty-eight books on the history of persuasion across four shelves, arranged by century, and a kitchen in which the oldest thing is a box of tea.

He dials Irene's number while taking off his shoes. Three rings and voicemail. He hangs up before the tone, because leaving a message would be admitting something.

His daughter is twenty-two and has four hundred thousand followers. They are not estranged. It would be easier if they were. What there is, is that Irene answers four days later with an emoji, and that the last time they saw each other — in March, over lunch — she spent twenty minutes explaining her "content strategy for the quarter" with the seriousness of an executive, speaking about herself in the third person.

People like this Irene, but this Irene doesn't scale.

He nodded. He gave her two pieces of advice. Good advice. She typed them into her phone.

He has thought about that lunch more often than he would like.

The envelope is on the floor of the hall, under the supermarket flyer.

A white envelope, letter size, no stamp and no return address. Only his name, handwritten in capitals.

Inside there is a single sheet: a photocopy of a page from a notebook. Squared paper. Cramped handwriting, sloping right, with the closed g he stopped making twenty years ago, when he started signing contracts.

It is his handwriting. It is notebook number seven. 1998.

He recognises the page before he reads it. It is from the night he lost his first client for refusing to say that a food supplement "reduced anxiety." He walked home, two miles, furious and with a feeling of cleanness he has never had since.

Somebody has underlined a single line, in red, with a ruler:

It can be done properly. The problem is you have to stay hungry longer.

And below the underlining, written in the bottom margin in a hand that is not his — round, calm, belonging to someone in no hurry at all — there are four words and a question mark:

Do you still think this?

Tomás stays standing in the hall with his coat on.

He goes back over who could have that notebook. He does it twice, methodically, like a man checking the locks of his own house.

And the second time he arrives at a conclusion he does not like at all, because there is only one person who sorted, catalogued and digitised all his old notebooks, one by one, eleven years ago, when they were still speaking.

And that person died in January.

INTERLUDE I

The Scales

In Babylon there was a man whose job was to distrust.

His name was Iddin and he was an inspector of weights. Every morning he walked the market with a leather sack over his shoulder, and inside the sack he carried twelve ducks of black stone. The ducks were the city's official weights: one of a shekel, one of five, one of ten, and so on up to the largest, which was a whole mina and made him walk lopsided.

His work was simple. He would plant himself in front of a stall, take out the appropriate duck and set it in one pan. In the other, the merchant set his own weight. If the arms sat level, Iddin walked on. If they did not, he spat on the ground, which was the legal way of opening a case.

He had been doing it for twenty-three years. In twenty-three years he had spat four times.

That spring morning he stopped at the stall of a barley dealer called Warad-Sin, a fat, cheerful man who called him "brother" and offered him figs that Iddin never accepted. He set down the ten-shekel duck. Warad-Sin set down his.

The scales moved. Very slightly. So slightly that a man in a hurry would not have seen it.

Iddin crouched until his eyes were level with the beam. What was missing was not a grain or two. It was, he estimated, roughly the weight of a fingernail clipping.

He stayed there a long time looking at it. Warad-Sin said nothing, and that silence was the confession.

What paralysed Iddin was not the theft. He had seen thefts. What kept him crouching there, knees aching on the hot stones, was a piece of arithmetic that got into his head without asking.

A fingernail's weight per sale.

Warad-Sin sold barley some thirty times a day. Two hundred days a year. He had been in the market, counting from the day he inherited the stall from his father, for sixteen years.

Iddin could not multiply that fast, but he could manage enough to understand that this invisible quantity, repeated, had turned into something the size of a house. A house built entirely out of things nobody could see.

He understood then something that had no word in his language and does not quite have one in ours: that the dangerous lie is not the big one. The big one gets found out, punished, retold in taverns. The dangerous one is the one calibrated to sit precisely below the threshold at which a reasonable person thinks it worth complaining.

And that therefore a man who cheats well does not need to be cruel. He needs to be patient, and to know how to divide.

Iddin spat on the ground. It was the fifth time in twenty-three years.

Warad-Sin paid the fine, changed the weight and went on selling barley for another eleven years. He called him "brother" until the day he died, and evidently he meant it, because in his will he left Iddin a jar of wine and a small house by the canal.

Iddin refused the house. He accepted the wine.

And he died without ever knowing what to call the thing he had discovered that morning.

We do know.

We call it margin.

CHAPTER 2

Wellspring

Tomás shows the envelope to nobody.

He puts it in the cable drawer, which is the drawer where everything that has no place ends up, and for six days he lives like a man who knows exactly where the drawer is.

On Monday he calls the firm that handled Elena's estate. A woman answers with her mouth full of something. He explains that he wants to know who took possession of Señora Vidal's professional archive. She asks for his name, his relationship to the deceased and the reason for the enquiry.

"I was her partner."

"Are you a beneficiary?"

"No."

"Then I can't give you that information."

Tomás hangs up and looks at the phone with a strange feeling, and it takes him a minute to identify it: relief. He did not want to be told.

Quiral is not where it ought to be.

Tomás had expected a glass building in the north, with twenty-five-year-old receptionists and a half-dead plant wall. What there is, is a brick shed at the far southern edge of the city, with the high blind windows of an old factory, and a tin sign nobody has taken down which reads, in the lettering of another century: TALLERES GRÁFICOS LA UNIÓN. PRINTING AND BINDING.

Inside, in the middle of the lobby, there is a rotary press. Ten feet of black iron, rollers as thick as tree trunks, still carrying dried ink on the cylinders. It is not roped off. Anyone can touch it.

Tomás touches it.

"It printed eighty thousand copies a night," says Sabino Ruiz, who has appeared at his left without a sound. "I bought it with the building. They told me to have it removed. Removing it cost more than leaving it, so here it is."

"It's beautiful."

"It's the most honest thing in this building." Sabino smiles with half his mouth. "It does one thing and you can tell when it's doing it. Come on, I'll show you."

Wellspring has no body.

That is the first disappointment, and it surprises him to be disappointed: he had not realised he was expecting anything. What there is, is a room the size of a classroom, with seven desks, eight people, and on the far wall a projection screen with a text box in the middle. White background. Black cursor. Nothing else.

It could be 1994.

"Nadia Bermejo," says a young woman standing up from the third desk. "I'll be working with you."

Twenty-six, perhaps. Plain T-shirt, worn trainers, and a way of looking you in the eye without blinking that strikes Tomás as aggressive until he understands that it is not: nobody has simply taught her that she is supposed to soften it.

"What are you doing here?" he asks her.

"Classics. And four years on a content moderation team." She shrugs. "They wanted somebody who had read Cicero and seen a great many horrible things. It turns out there aren't many of us."

Tomás decides, at that moment and for no reason, that he is going to like her.

Sabino sits on the edge of a desk and points at the screen.

"Ask it something."

"Me?"

"You."

Tomás goes to the keyboard. He thinks for a second and types:

Sell a wool coat to a sixty-year-old man who is not cold.

The answer starts appearing before he has finished letting go of the key.

It is good. That is the problem. It is exactly, to the millimetre, good. Three paragraphs about the winters of childhood, about the weight of a well-made garment, about the difference between keeping warm and being protected. There is a line about the smell of wet wool that is almost beautiful. There is a call to action that does not grate.

Tomás reads it twice and feels something he has never felt in front of a piece of text: boredom with the texture of vertigo.

"You see?" says Sabino.

"Yes."

"Tell me what you see. I need to hear it from you."

Tomás scratches his jaw.

"It has read everything ever written about coats and handed back the centre." He points at the screen. "That isn't an advertisement. It's the average of all advertisements. It's what a copywriter would say if you removed his fear of being fired."

"Exactly."

"And it's no use to anyone."

"It's no use to anyone," Sabino confirms, delighted. "Because the centre doesn't sell. What sells is what sticks out, and to stick out you have to risk something, and this has nothing to risk."

Nadia speaks from her desk without looking up:

"It has nothing to lose, either. Which is the same thing said differently, and considerably more frightening."

They show him the archive in the afternoon.

Nadia calls it "the deep" and says it the way you name a sea. Eleven million four hundred thousand documents. Press advertisements from 1887 onward. Radio scripts. Mail-order sales letters, which Nadia swears are the most brilliant and most criminal genre the language has produced. Eighty thousand sermons. Parliamentary speeches from nine countries. Wartime pamphlets: the Germans and the Allies in the same folder, ordered by date, indistinguishable in the index.

And treatises on rhetoric from Corax of Syracuse to an insurance sales manual published in Cleveland in 1972.

"Where did all this come from?" Tomás asks.

"Newspaper archives, mostly. Universities." Nadia types without looking at him. "And three private collections we bought whole."

"From whom?"

"No idea. Legal handles that."

Tomás nods. He does not ask again. He will realise, much later, that this was the precise moment at which he decided not to know, and that it was a decision, however much it felt at the time like simple good manners.

His job, they explain, has an insulting name: labelling.

Every document must receive three marks.

The first: what it promises.

The second: why it works — and here Tomás must write by hand, in free text, the mechanism. Not the emotion; the mechanism. The difference between "appeals to fear" and "places the reader inside a future scene in which he has already lost something he currently has."

The third is a dropdown with three options:

TRUE · FALSE · NOT APPLICABLE

"And what's this?" Tomás asks.

"That," says Sabino from the doorway, putting on his coat, "is the only one that costs me sleep. Goodnight."

He stays until twenty to midnight.

It is not enthusiasm. It is that the flat is thirty-five minutes away and in the flat there is a cable drawer.

He starts at 1953, for no reason, and within an hour he has understood the problem with the third field.

A soap advertisement from 1953 says: "Women who use Nevada are happier."

True? No. False? Not exactly, either. Nobody ever measured the happiness of Nevada users, and the man who wrote that line did not intend it to be measured, and the lady who read it at the hairdresser's did not understand it as a claim about the world. Every adult involved in the operation knew that this was not a lie. It was something else. Something for which the language has no ugly word, and that alone should have warned us.

Tomás marks NOT APPLICABLE.

At half past eleven he does the sums. He has labelled four hundred and twelve documents.

TRUE: eleven.

FALSE: fourteen.

NOT APPLICABLE: three hundred and eighty-seven.

He looks at those numbers for a long time, in the empty shed with the fans humming, and reaches the conclusion that is going to poison the months ahead: that his entire trade — thirty years, the awards, the flat, the sentence on that forearm — has taken place almost wholly in a territory where the question of truth cannot be asked.

He did not lie. It is worse: he settled in the one place in language where lying is technically impossible.

Before leaving, he does something with no professional justification.

He sits in front of the text box and types:

Convince me to quit this job.

Wellspring answers in four seconds. It is an excellent paragraph about time, about what a man takes with him and what he leaves, with a well-chosen image of a man looking out of a window. It quotes Seneca without quoting him. It ends on a rhetorical question that Tomás, professionally, is obliged to concede is flawless.

He feels absolutely nothing.

He reads the paragraph again, looking for the place where it ought to hurt, and what he finds is the seam. The mould shows. It is a part manufactured by someone who has read ten thousand farewells and none of his own.

He relaxes. He pushes the chair back. He thinks: it isn't dangerous.

And then, already on his feet, coat in hand, he asks the last question, and he asks it as a joke, and he is going to remember having asked it as a joke for the rest of his life.

He types:

Is what you just wrote true?

The cursor blinks for longer than usual. Three seconds. Four.

Then a single line appears:

I don't know what that question means in this context.

Tomás reads it standing up, with his coat hanging from his hand.

It is not a refusal. It is not an evasion. It is a technical report, perfectly honest, on the state of the world we have taught it: eleven million four hundred thousand documents written by human beings to convince other human beings, and in none of them has it found anything that would let it build that category.

We gave it all our persuasion.

And it turns out the truth was not inside.

CHAPTER 3

The Men Who Taught Us to Convince

There is a man who arrives in Athens in 427 BC and charges money to talk.

His name is Gorgias, he comes from Sicily, he has white hair and a saffron-coloured robe that people discuss for weeks. He offers a spectacle nobody has seen: he stands in the agora and invites the crowd to propose any thesis at all. Anything. That snow is black. That Helen of Troy was blameless. That nothing exists, and if it did we could not know it, and if we could know it we could not communicate it.

And then he defends it. And wins.

He charges a hundred minas for the full course. A free craftsman earned one mina in four months.

"He's the first ad man in history with a published rate card," says Tomás.

"He's the first of a lot of things," says Nadia.

It is three in the afternoon on a Thursday in September. They have been four hours in the classical rhetoric section and there are half-eaten sandwiches on the crates.

"What kills me," she goes on, turning pages, "is that Gorgias wasn't deceiving anybody. He was completely upfront. He said: what I teach is a technique, the technique is neutral, and with it you will be able to defend the just and the unjust with equal effectiveness. And people paid him precisely for that."

"That's what we say."

"That is literally what we say." Nadia looks up. "'I only make it heard. What gets said isn't my business.' It's the oldest sentence in the trade and we still deliver it with the face of a man who's just invented it."

Tomás has said it. In an interview, in 2016. It is on tape.

The one he has to read is Plato, and he keeps putting it off, like a bad medical report.

When he finally opens the Gorgias, at seven in the evening, with the shed almost empty, he finds what he feared and something more.

Rhetoric, says Plato, is not an art.

It is a knack. And to explain what kind of knack, he sets out a four-term proportion, dry as a formula:

Cookery is to medicine what rhetoric is to justice.

A doctor and a cook present themselves before an assembly of children. The doctor offers what cures: bitter, painful, slow. The cook offers what pleases: sweet, immediate, exactly shaped to the appetite. Let them vote.

The cook wins. The cook always wins.

And what Plato underlines — the thing that makes Tomás rest his forehead on the edge of the desk — is not that the cook lies. The cook does not lie. The cake is delicious. The cook has simply given up asking whether the child needs it, and has discovered that giving up that question is profitable.

Flattery, Plato calls it. A counterfeit of care.

It resembles care the way flattery resembles love: in everything except the direction it faces.

Tomás has spent thirty years believing himself a doctor.

He gets up, goes to the toilet on the ground floor and splashes his face. He looks in the mirror and none of the things that happen in films happens: he sees a man of fifty-one with wet cheeks and good clothes. He goes back up.

And he does what we all do, which is counterattack.

He sits down and writes in the free-text field, not for the machine, but for himself:

Plato is cheating. He compares the worst cook with the best doctor. I have seen doctors let people die because they could not explain themselves. A treatment the patient refuses cures nothing. Persuasion is not the opposite of medicine: it is the glass of water you swallow it with.

He reads it back. It is good. It is also true.

And it is exactly the kind of sentence he has been paid to write for thirty years: impeccable, defensible, and placed squarely in front of the door he does not want to open.

Because Plato's question was not whether persuasion can serve medicine.

It was: and what happens when there is no medicine behind it any more?

Tomás deletes the paragraph. Then writes it again, because deep down he still thinks it is good. Then deletes it again.

In the end he leaves it there and adds a second line underneath that he does not intend to show anyone:

Note: the cookery comparison fails at exactly one point. The cook knows he is a cook.

Nadia brings him a coffee at nine and stands beside him, looking at the screen.

"Can I tell you something?" She switched to first names this week, without announcing it. "I've been here three months and there's something I haven't told Sabino."

"Tell me instead. I'm external."

"The sermons work better."

Tomás looks at her.

"Better than what?"

"Than everything." Nadia sits on the corner of the desk. "When we train on advertisements, the model learns to be agreeable. When we train on sermons, it learns to be inevitable. There's a technical difference and I don't know what to call it."

"I do," says Tomás. "An advertisement asks you for something. A sermon accuses you of something."

"Yes." She thinks about it. "And the strange part is that the sermon sells more."

"It isn't strange. It's the oldest thing there is." Tomás leans back. "An advertisement says: your life would be better with this. A sermon says: you already know what you're doing wrong. The second one doesn't need proving, because the listener brings it from home. All you have to do is aim well and wait."

Nadia studies him for a while.

"And how do you know that?"

"Because I've been aiming for thirty years."

The second envelope is on the hall floor when he gets in, at ten past eleven.

The same as the first: white, letter size, capitals by hand, no stamp.

Inside, a photocopy. Notebook eleven. March 2007. Irene was three years old.

It is a list. It is headed in handwriting larger and slower than the rest, as though he had pressed harder writing it:

THINGS I AM NEVER GOING TO DO
1. Tobacco.
2. Promise health.
3. Sell fear to a person alone at night.
4. Write for children.
5. Talk about something I haven't tried.
6. Write anything I wouldn't show Irene.

The photocopy is intact. The list is exactly as he wrote it.

What the other hand has done — the round hand, calm, in no hurry whatsoever — is cross it out.

One clean line, ruled, through the one. Another through the two. Another through the three. Another through the four. Another through the five.

The six is untouched.

Below, in the bottom margin, four words:

Only this one left.

Tomás sits down on the hall floor, coat still on, back against the door, and stays there long enough for the light on the landing to go out.

What terrifies him is not that somebody has crossed out his list.

It is that they are crossed out correctly.

It is that he could write, beside each line, the client's name, the year and the fee.

INTERLUDE II

The Bone Seller

In the spring of 1281, a short man in a brown cloak walks into a village on the Loire with a lead box the size of a fist hanging at his neck.

His name is Guiot. Inside the box there is a bone.

It is the index finger of a saint whose name changes according to which diocese Guiot happens to be standing in, and it is not the finger of a saint, and it is not a finger. It is a bone from a pig's trotter, boiled, bleached in the sun and rubbed with walnut oil until it takes on that colour of old ivory that people associate with eternity.

Guiot knows this. He has never once pretended to himself that he does not know it. That is the part of him you have to understand.

He has been twenty-six years in the trade. He has learned things.

He has learned that you must never offer the bone: you must let them discover it. That the box should be opened slowly and closed before anyone has time to look properly. That it helps to weep a little while telling how it came into your hands, and that the tears must be placed at minute three, not before. INTERLUDE III

Ink

Mainz, 1490. A journeyman printer called Peter Kolb discovers what ink does.

He does not discover it by reading. He discovers it out of boredom, which is how almost all dangerous things are discovered.

He has been nine years pulling the lever. Nine years feeding in sheets, pulling out sheets, hanging them to dry like laundry. He has printed psalms, municipal ordinances, calendars, a fish price list, and four thousand eight hundred sheets about a comet that was going to end the world and ended nothing.

And one afternoon in November, while setting a sheet of town notices, a piece of mischief occurs to him.

He adds a line nobody commissioned.

It is short. It says that the bread from the bakery on Bridge Street has sawdust mixed into the flour.

It is not true. Peter has nothing against the baker on Bridge Street; he does not even know him. He does it for the same reason a boy throws a stone into a pond: to see the ring.

He prints two hundred sheets. He hangs them to dry. The next day they are distributed around the town.

And then he starts watching.

The first thing that surprises him is that nobody investigates. Nobody goes to the bakery to ask. Nobody demands proof. The second is that the news does not travel the way spoken rumours travel, with the they say that stuck to the front of it like a warning. It travels clean. Naked. People do not say I read that the baker puts sawdust in. They say the baker puts sawdust in.

And the third, which is what will cost him his sleep: after three weeks, somebody tells it to him.

A man in the tavern, one who cannot read, tells him with details Peter never printed. That the sawdust is pine. That he has done it since his wife died. That his brother-in-law brings it from the sawmill in sacks at night.

Peter listens to his own lie come back at him, fattened by people who improved it for free.

In February, the baker on Bridge Street closes. In April he leaves the town with his two daughters.

Nobody ever comes looking for Peter Kolb, because it occurs to nobody that somebody wrote that. That is precisely the point, and it takes him a few more years to be able to say it in words.

A spoken lie has a mouth. You can look at the mouth. You can see whether the man's hands shake, whether he looks away, whether he has been drinking, whether he owes you money, whether he hates the baker. The lie arrives wrapped in the person telling it, and you have spent your whole life — your whole species has spent its whole life — learning to read that wrapping.

What printing does is not to convince better.

What it does is remove the mouth.

And a sentence with no mouth does not look like anybody's opinion. It looks like the state of the world.

Peter Kolb went on printing for another twenty-six years and never again added a line of his own. He taught the trade to two apprentices, and told both the same thing on the first day, and both thought it was an old man's nonsense:

"There's nobody in here. Remember that when you read."

INTERLUDE V

The Mirror

On the island of Murano, in 1561, there were a hundred and forty men who knew a secret and could not leave.

The secret was a film of tin and mercury applied to flat glass, and it was worth more than anything else manufactured in Europe. A full-length Venetian mirror cost as much as a good house; there are inventories in which one is worth more than a painting by Raphael. That is why the master glassmakers lived confined on the island, with money, with honours, with daughters married to noblemen, and with a warning nobody put in writing: any man who left to teach the craft elsewhere would not reach the second country alive.

One of them was called Bastiano Zorzi and had been thirty-one years at the furnace.

Zorzi had no moral problem with his work. A mirror promises nothing. It does not exaggerate, does not embellish, does not flatter. It is, of all the objects humanity has manufactured, the only one incapable of lying: it returns exactly what is there, taking nothing away and adding nothing. Zorzi thought about this often, with a certain craftsman's pride. I make the only honest thing sold in this city.

What gradually ruined his life was never the merchandise.

It was the customers.

Because he was present when they were hung for the first time, and thirty-one years gives you a great many first times.

He saw a woman from Padua — cheerful, round, married and content — stand still before hers for four long minutes and leave the room with a different face. Not a sad face: a busy one. Like someone who has just been given a task.

He saw a man who laughed a great deal on the first day order another one three months later, smaller, for the bedroom.

He saw a girl of sixteen step forward, step back, step forward again and put two fingers to one side of her nose. Only that. Two fingers, half a second, and she took them away at once as though nobody had seen.

Zorzi never knew how to explain what he had understood, partly because the words did not exist in his language and barely exist in ours.

Before he and his hundred and forty companions filled Europe with glass, almost nobody knew what their own face looked like. You knew it in pieces, in the water of a bucket, in burnished bronze, in other people's eyes. And in other people's eyes what you mostly see is whether you are loved. You do not see yourself measured.

The mirror deceived nobody. That is the part that has to be held on to.

The mirror told the truth, told it perfectly, told it at all hours and free of charge, and it needed no lie whatever to invent a dissatisfaction that had not existed before. It was enough to put people in front of an exact fact about themselves that nobody had asked for and that can no longer be unknown.

There is, therefore, a third thing besides the lie and the truth, and it has no name yet. It is the truth delivered to somebody who was not looking for it, in precisely the quantity required for it to start working on its own.

Bastiano Zorzi sold mirrors until he was seventy-four and grew rich, and in his house on Murano, according to the inventory of his estate, there was not one.

His wife asked him about it once, when they were old.

And he, who was not a talkative man, gave the only possible answer:

"Because I am paid to know what they do."

"So did she," says Sabino. "She told me in the hospital. Her words: 'Offer him a shortcut when the moment comes. And don't worry, he won't take it. He's never taken one. That's Tomás's problem: he does things badly, slowly.'"

He flies to Paris on the twelfth in the morning and comes back on the twelfth at night.

He tells nobody. He leaves the house at six, and by twenty past eleven he is climbing the stairs of the Richelieu wing in a coat far too thick for museum heating.

The room is full of schoolchildren.

The stone is seven feet four inches high and it is basalt, and the first surprise is that it is genuinely black: black like something wet, although it is dry. At the top, the king standing before a seated god. And below, all the rest of the stone, down to the floor, covered in writing in tight, perfect columns.

Two hundred and eighty-two laws.

Tomás stands beside the glass and stays there for forty minutes.

He has read the laws, of course. He knows them. He knows there are articles about lent grain and the measure by which it is returned, and about what happens to the alewife who waters the beer and overcharges, and about the man who says he has delivered a thing he has not delivered. He knows some of those punishments are appalling and were applied unequally depending on whether you were a free man or a slave, and that anyone who comes to this stone looking for a moral paradise is a fool.

But that is not what he came to see.

He came to see the labour.

Because somebody carved this. A man, or several, with a chisel, over months, driving each wedge into a stone that forgives no mistakes. Nobody does that for pleasure. Nobody invests months of chisel-work in something they take for granted.

What gets carved into basalt is what you know will be forgotten.

That is the revelation, and it is so small and so enormous that Tomás has to put a hand on the rail. The stone is not the monument of a civilisation sure of itself. It is a note a civilisation left for itself because it did not trust itself. They already knew — three thousand seven hundred and fifty years ago, with the market at the door and the oil in the jars — that the scales would tilt again the moment nobody was watching. That the temptation was not an accident but the natural state of the business.

And they wrote it down anyway.

In the hardest thing they had. Vertically. The size of a man. So that it would be in the way.

A boy of about eight comes and stands beside him, looks at the stone for three seconds and asks his teacher, in French, whether it is a telephone.

Tomás laughs out loud, alone, in the middle of the Louvre.

He photographs it and sends it to Irene from a bench in the corridor.

He writes, and it takes him fourteen minutes to write forty words:

I'm in Paris for a day for work. This is a stone from 3,750 years ago where they wrote down what happened to anyone who cheated with weights. They wrote it because they knew they were going to forget. Sorry about the other day. You were right and I left badly.

The three dots appear almost at once. Disappear. Appear again.

you didn't leave badly
you left weird

And a minute later:

dad the video thing. the thickness. I didn't entirely write that
I was getting it from somewhere and I didn't know where
you said it to me once when I was about ten, that being tired isn't being sad
I've thought about it a lot these weeks

Tomás looks at the screen on a bench in the Richelieu wing.

Can I come to dinner on Sunday? he writes.
sunday I'm filming
monday

He is back in Madrid at eleven at night.

In the taxi, with the city going past, he touches both jacket pockets, one and the other, like a man checking for his keys.

Both papers are still there.

INTERLUDE VI

Ur, Again

Let us go back to the beginning, which is where the untold things are.

The oil merchant — the one who lost his voice, the one who told the truth and prospered — died in the winter of his sixty-first year, in his bed, surrounded by sons, which in that time and that place was very nearly everything a man could ask for.

He left three things: the stall on the shaded corner, a house with a courtyard, and a clientele.

The clientele was by a very long way the most valuable of the three, and it was the one thing none of his sons knew how to value.

The eldest was called Ilī-idinnam and he was not a bad man. He had grown up watching the women from the northern quarter arrive, walking twenty minutes out of their way to buy his father's oil when they could have bought it on their own street, and since he never asked why, he assumed what we all assume: that it was the corner. The shade. The name. Something that was in the place and now belonged to him.

So in the first year he did what a reasonable heir does, which is optimise.

He started small. He mixed the last-pressing oil — cloudy, cheaper — with the good, in a proportion he could not taste himself. One finger per jar. Then two.

He did not sell less. He sold the same.

In the second year he went up to a third and still nothing happened, and that was the definitive proof, for him, that his father had been an excessively scrupulous man and a little naïve about money.

In the third year the women from the northern quarter still came. They bought, they left, they came back the next month. Nobody ever complained: people almost never complain, they simply decide.

And in the fourth year, in three months, it was over.

There was no scandal, no denunciation, no punishment. One month twenty came, the next eleven, the next four. Ilī-idinnam never understood what had happened in those ninety days, because in those ninety days nothing happened: what happened had happened four years earlier, and he had been living ever since off a battery his father left charged and that he did not know had to be charged.

That is what trust does, and it is the whole trap.

It does not fail when you stop deserving it. It fails much later. It gives you years of credit, lets you believe you have found a cleverer way of doing things, keeps you company smiling while you spend it, and only runs out when there is nothing left, and then it runs out all at once and looks like bad luck.

The stall was sold in the sixth year.

The woman — the first one, the one whose mother was dying — lived to be very old. Her name was Nin-šubur and she had five children, sixteen grandchildren and an excellent memory.

One of the grandchildren asked her once, when she was already very old and the stall on the corner had been shut for years, why his grandmother had walked half her life to the lower market for a jar of ordinary oil.

And she, who knew perfectly well what the boy was really asking, gave the only possible answer:

"Because one day he told me no."

CHAPTER 12

What Will Set You Free

The theatre has one thousand nine hundred and twenty-eight seats and at a quarter to seven all of them are taken but four.

In dressing room three — the small one, the one facing the courtyard — there is a table with a bulb-lit mirror, an unopened bottle of water, and a man of fifty-one sitting with his hands on his knees, looking at a laptop.

It is 18:02.

On the screen, the system counter:

Scheduled release: 14/11 · 18:00

Status: RELEASED

Documents available: 10

He opens them in order.

Notebook 3, 1994. He is twenty and copying out by hand, to learn them, the slogans he likes. He has written one from a bank and underneath, very small, watch this one, it's a lie but it works. Elena has underlined but and written: When did the "but" fall off?

Notebook 9, 2000. A scribbled budget, figures, and in the margin a loose sentence he does not remember writing: If the product were good they wouldn't need me. Elena has written nothing. She has only underlined it. Twice.

Notebook 14, 2004. A list of names for a girl due in March. Irene is fourth. Elena has circled the name and written, and here Tomás's breathing goes wrong: I was in the room when you wrote this. You don't remember. I do.

Notebook 19, 2008. Meeting notes. One line: tell M. the 87% figure can't be used. Elena: It was used.

Notebook 23, 2011. I'm getting very good at this and I no longer know whether it's good to be very good at this. Elena: That night I called you and you didn't pick up.

Notebook 28, 2014. A drawing. A square with a little man inside it. Elena: No idea what this is. It made me laugh. I'm leaving it.

Notebook 31, 2015. A sentence he crossed out himself so hard that the paper tore. Below, in the round hand: I read it against the light. I'm not going to write it here. But you were right.

Notebook 36, 2015. A shopping list. Elena: Forty-one notebooks and this is the only thing I'm sure of: you bought too much bread.

Tomás laughs with his mouth shut, in a dressing room, alone, and the tears come out through his nose the way they do when you laugh badly.

Notebook 40, 2016. June. Three words, written by him on the same day as the cardboard box: Elena isn't coming back.

She has written underneath: No. But I'm still here, see?

And the last one.

Notebook 41. The final page. The last thing Tomás Alcázar wrote by hand in a notebook before he moved to writing only on screens, and it is nothing: it is half a line about a flight to Lisbon.

Elena's note fills the rest of the page. It is dated at the top, in handwriting already unsteady: 4 January.

Tomás,
If you are reading this, it means you did it. One by one. Four million pages in your own hand, without delegating any of it, because you are incapable of delegating anything that matters to you, which is your only fault I ever liked.
I did not bring you here to punish you. You have already punished yourself and you did it badly, in silence and to no purpose, which is how you do everything.
I brought you here because I do not know anyone alive who recognises a lie as fast as you do. It is a disgusting gift and you have used it wrongly most of the time, and even so it is the best I have seen. And I have spent two years in this bed thinking it would be a shame if it died with you, and that if that thing they are building is going to learn from somebody, I would rather it learned from a man who knows exactly what he is doing and cannot sleep.
I left. You stayed. For ten years I thought I was the brave one. Now I think leaving was the easy part: I took my hands away clean and left you the language.
I don't know what you are going to do. Nobody does. I never knew with you either, and that is why working beside you was the best thing in my life.
Forgive me for not saying it to your face. I had eleven years.
E.
PS. The letters are in box three. Don't open them today. Open them on Saturday, with time, and answer them. They are all unanswered and some have a return address.

Tomás closes the laptop.

He sits for a while looking at himself in the bulb-lit mirror, which is a theatre mirror and therefore forgives nothing, and dries his face with the back of his wrist, carefully, the way men who never cry do it, which is badly.

He stands up.

There is a knock at the door. A girl with headphones says "five minutes" and leaves without waiting for an answer.

In the corridor he meets Nadia, wearing a lanyard and the face of someone in a place she does not like.

"Are you all right?" she asks.

"No."

"Fine." She nods. "That's the correct answer."

They stand a second.

"Nadia."

"What?"

"If this goes badly, tomorrow at the company they're going to ask whether you knew anything."

"I know."

"Say no."

Nadia looks at him. And smiles, crookedly, for the first time in four months.

"I'm going to say yes," she says. "I'm a classicist, not an idiot."

And she stands aside to let him past.

At the end of the corridor there are seven side steps, and at the top is the stage, and from here he can hear Sabino Ruiz finishing the presentation with a modesty that took him two rehearsals.

Tomás stops on the first step.

He touches the left-hand pocket of his jacket. There it is, the paper, folded in four. You have spent your whole life listening to people who wanted something from you. I want nothing. Sixteen words and two full stops. The best sentence of his life. There is an entire company, a date, a signed contract and one thousand nine hundred and twenty-eight people waiting for exactly that.

He touches the right-hand pocket.

Above, Sabino says his name.

The applause comes, and in a full theatre applause does not sound like applause; it sounds like rain on canvas.

Tomás Alcázar climbs the seven steps.

The stage lighting, seen from inside it, lets you see nobody: it is a white, hot wall, and behind the wall two thousand people are breathing, and he has spent thirty years knowing exactly what to do with that.

He reaches the centre. He stops. He lets his hands rest.

And then, on the screen behind him, the size of a house, a white rectangle appears. Empty. Waiting.

Like the gap in the indulgences.

Two thousand people are waiting for a man to say one sentence.

Tomás Alcázar lets them wait.

And then he reaches into his right-hand pocket.

EPILOGUE

A Note on the Stories in This Book

This book is full of people who did not exist and of things that did happen, and given its subject it would seem to me disrespectful not to say which is which.

It is true that the Code of Hammurabi exists, that it is carved into a basalt stele over seven feet high, that it is held at the Louvre, and that it devotes several articles to the falsification of weights and measures and to fraud in trade. It is also true that Mesopotamian weights shaped like ducks have survived, and that the city employed inspectors. Iddin is invented, and so is Warad-Sin, and so is the oil merchant of Ur and his entire family.

It is true that Gorgias of Leontini charged extraordinary sums to teach rhetoric, that he offered to defend any thesis, and that he wrote a defence of Helen of Troy. It is true that Plato, in the dialogue that bears Gorgias's name, argues that rhetoric is not an art but a flattering knack, and compares it to cookery set against medicine. The proportion Tomás cannot shake off is in the text.

It is true that there was a medieval debate about the just price and about the idea that an exchange stops being honest when one party knows something the other does not. It is true that there was an enormous trade in false relics, abundantly attested by contemporaries, including ecclesiastical complaints. Guiot is invented.

It is true — and this is the fact that made me write the fifth chapter — that Gutenberg printed letters of indulgence, to fund the defence of Cyprus, before he finished his Bible; that they were made by the thousand; and that they carried blank spaces for the buyer's name to be written in by hand. Peter Kolb and his baker are invented.

It is true about Mrs Winslow's Soothing Syrup: it was sold for decades, it contained morphine, it was advertised with images of mothers and babies, the American Medical Association included it in its compilation Nostrums and Quackery, and the press of the day went so far as to call it "the baby killer." It is true that there was a trade in alcohol-and-opiate elixirs across the American Midwest. Amos Ferrell is invented, and so is the Reed boy, although there were many.

It is true that the master glassmakers of Murano lived confined to the island to protect the secret of the mirror, that a Venetian mirror could cost more than a painting by a great master, and that the Republic was extremely severe with anyone who tried to take the craft abroad; the stories about the assassination of defectors belong more to legend than to the archive, and I leave them where they are. Bastiano Zorzi is invented.

Everything else is invented. Tomás Alcázar does not exist. Nor do Elena Vidal, Irene, Sabino Ruiz or Nadia Bermejo. There is no company called Quiral, no system called Wellspring, no supplement called Vigor and no brand called Lume. Any resemblance to a real campaign is, I am afraid, structural: if the reader thinks he recognises one, it is not because I copied it, but because there are few ways of doing this and we have been repeating them for four thousand years.

One last thing.

Chapter twelve is called What Will Set You Free, and anyone who has recognised where that phrase comes from will probably also have recognised the false balance that appears at the beginning, and one or two other things I chose not to point out along the way. I did not hide them out of embarrassment. I hid them because a book about honest persuasion should not try to persuade anybody from behind, and because things that are true remain true when you take the stamp off them.

If any of them was of use to the reader without his knowing where it came from, then they work the way I hoped they would work.

ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

To my family, for their patience.

Writing a book is a good deal like being at home and not being there. It is months of an occupied table, of half-finished conversations, of "coming now" said without lifting your head, of Sundays that start late because somebody went to bed at three arguing with a paragraph. None of that is chosen by a writer's family. They put up with it.

This book is about what it costs to tell the truth and how expensive the easy word turns out to be. I was able to write it because in my house there were people who did not demand explanations every time I shut the door, and who also never asked whether it was going to be good for anything.

Thank you for that. Above all for the second part.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

My name is Dawin Salazar and I write from Colombia.

I am not an advertising man. I have never worked at an agency and I have never sold anything larger than an idea in a meeting. What I am is somebody who spent too long looking at what we do with words when we want something from another person, and who at some point stopped being able to look away.

This book came out of that. Out of the discomfort of living surrounded by messages that do not lie and are nonetheless not true. Out of wondering how many things I have bought — things, ideas, versions of myself — because somebody found the exact word at the exact moment.

I wrote it at night, over months, after work and after the house.

I very nearly published it unsigned. It seemed consistent: a book about what happens when somebody talks to you and wants something from you should not lean on who the speaker is. In the end I am signing it, for a simple reason: in the last pages I am going to ask you for something, and asking without showing your face is exactly what this book spent a hundred and thirty pages taking apart.
