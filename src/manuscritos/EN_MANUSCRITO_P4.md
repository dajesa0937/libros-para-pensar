## CHAPTER 8
### The Thirst

The commission, said aloud, is ridiculous.

On the fourteenth of November, at seven in the evening, in a converted theatre in the centre of town, Quiral is going to show Wellspring to the world. There will be press, investors, a government minister. And at some point in that hour the screen at the back will go blank and the first sentence the machine addresses to humanity will appear, letter by letter.

It has to seem to be its own.

Tomás has to write it.

"It's a fairground trick," he says to Sabino in October.

"Everything is."

"No, it's literally a fairground trick. The dummy talks and the ventriloquist stands behind."

"Yes." Sabino nods, calmly. "And we have been paying to see the dummy for four thousand years, knowing there is a man behind it, and our blood still runs cold when the dummy looks at us. Don't despise it, Tomás. It's the oldest art form there is."

---

Tomás works for three weeks and nothing comes.

It is the first time in his life this has happened, and it is humiliating in a new way, because it is not a block: he writes every day, he writes a great deal, and everything he writes is good. That is the problem. What comes out is the standard of a highly competent man about to be paid a great deal of money.

> *I carry inside me everything you have said to one another.*

Good. Menacing, elegant, useless: it puts the audience on the defensive in second one.

> *I am not new. I am the oldest thing you have, finally in order.*

Better. Literate. Too literate: half the room will feel stupid, and people who feel stupid do not buy.

> *Hello.*

He laughs alone in the empty shed when he writes it. Then he looks at it for a while, because for a few minutes it seems to him it might be brilliant, and then he understands that it seems brilliant because it is night.

The problem, he decides on the twenty-seventh of October at two in the morning, is not talent.

The problem is that he does not know what he is selling.

A bottle of magnesium was easy: you sold absolution. A car: belonging. Insurance: the illusion that there is a version of the future you can buy in advance.

What does a machine that knows how to convince sell?

And the moment he asks the question properly, the answer arrives in under a second, and it is the worst night of his professional life, because good ideas do not arrive when you look for them: they arrive when you have finally taken away everything that was covering them.

What has to be sold is not what the machine does.

It is what the machine does *not* want.

---

He writes it on the back of a taxi receipt, at eleven minutes past two, and never touches it again:

> **"You have spent your whole life listening to people who wanted something from you. I want nothing."**

---

He looks at the scrap of paper.

It is perfect.

That is not an opinion: it is a valuation. Tomás Alcázar has spent thirty years assessing other people's sentences by the hour and he knows one when he sees it, even his own, and what he has in front of him does five things at once and not one of them shows.

First: it does not talk about the product. Not one word about what the machine is or does.

Second: it asks for nothing. And therefore it disables the only defence a human being carries as standard, which is the suspicion that the speaker wants something.

Third: it is true. Literally, technically, verifiable in a courtroom. The machine wants nothing. It has nothing to want with. Any lawyer in the world would sign that sentence.

Fourth: it is a lie. Absolutely. Because the speaker is not the machine. The speaker is a company with investors, and the machine is the mouth. The sentence is Peter Kolb's operation executed with five hundred years of refinement: it is not that they remove the author, it is that they put on top of him an author incapable of having interests.

And fifth — and this is the one that makes Tomás stand up and start pacing the corridor — the sentence consoles.

Because it is true that you have spent your whole life listening to people who wanted something from you. It is one of the truest things that can be said about a life. Your parents wanted something. Your boss. Your partner. The four thousand advertisements a day. And there is in that sentence, if you hear it tired, at eleven at night, a recognition so exact of how exhausting it is to exist among other people's interests that the listener's shoulders come down.

You are not tired. You have been strong for a very long time.

Twenty-five years later, the same hand, the same movement: first you absolve, and into the warm space you put the bottle.

He has done it again.

He has done it better than ever.

---

He shows it to Nadia on the Thursday, without saying whose it is.

"I need a cold read. It's from an external candidate."

Nadia reads the paper. She takes six seconds.

Then she does something Tomás was not expecting: she puts a hand to her breastbone. A small, involuntary gesture, of someone briefly out of air.

"Christ," she says.

"Good or bad?"

"No, it's that—" She reads it again. "It's that I believed it. I read it knowing perfectly well it's an advertisement, knowing I'm at the company that's paying for it, and I still believed it for one entire second." She looks up. "Who wrote it?"

"A man in Barcelona."

"Well, don't hire him." Nadia puts the paper down on the desk as though it were slightly hot. "Seriously. That isn't professional advice."

Tomás puts the taxi receipt in his inside pocket.

"Right," he says. "I'll bear it in mind."

---

There is another thing, in those weeks, and it is an absence.

No more envelopes come.

Tomás finds himself looking at the hall floor as he comes in, every night, with a disappointment he cannot justify. Three weeks. Four.

And one night, taking off his shoes, he finally does the arithmetic he has been avoiding for two months.

Elena annotated twelve pages.

Ten are locked in the system until the fourteenth of November.

Two were not.

He has received exactly two.

Which means that whoever posted them has not one envelope left to post. That they never had twelve. That they are not following a dead woman's plan: they are working with the little the system let them see.

Which means that whoever pushes those envelopes under his door is not a ghost.

It is an employee.

Tomás sits on the hall floor with one shoe in his hand and feels, for the first time since August, something very like relief, which it is not at all.

Because a ghost can only ask you questions.

A person can be right.

## CHAPTER 9
### What We Sold Irene

He goes up because she does not answer.

Four days, eleven messages, two calls. It is not alarming: Irene has gone whole weeks without answering. What is alarming is that he has gone there.

It is a building with no lift at number 34 of a narrow street, fourth floor, and when she opens the door the first thing she says is:

"Has something happened?"

"No."

"Oh." She stands aside. "Right."

Tomás walks into his daughter's home for the first time in two years and four months.

And it takes him about eight seconds to understand what he is looking at.

---

The flat is empty.

Not untidy: empty. A mattress on the bedroom floor, no bed frame, clothes folded in two towers beside it because there is no wardrobe. In the living room, nothing. Floor, walls, an unlit standard lamp, four cardboard boxes sealed with tape and a bicycle.

And then there are the three corners.

The first is to the left of the window: twenty square feet of wall painted a warm beige that is not the colour of the rest of the room, with a pale wooden shelf holding eleven books, a plant and a candle. In front of it, a green velvet armchair.

The second is the table in the corner, with a linen cloth, a vase and two mugs that do not match in a very studied way.

And the third is the kitchen.

Tomás approaches the kitchen the way you approach an accident. It is the kitchen from the Lume video. He recognises it frame by frame: the white tiles, the shelf with the three glass jars, the afternoon light coming in from the left.

The jars are full of dry pasta that has clearly been there a long time.

He opens the fridge without asking permission.

Inside there is a can of soft drink, half a lemon and fourteen cartons of a protein shake whose logo Tomás knows, because the agency worked for them in 2019.

"Dad."

"Sorry."

He shuts the fridge.

"I don't eat at home," says Irene, without the slightest embarrassment, leaning in the doorway. "I eat out or order in. It's cheaper than it looks if you know how."

Tomás looks again at the whole living room: the enormous emptiness and the three corners.

"And this?"

"Those are the sets." She says it with the naturalness with which you name a room. "That one's 'home in the morning'. The table is 'home with friends', though I barely use it. And the kitchen is the kitchen." She shrugs. "I painted that wall because beige performs better in afternoon light. The rest never appears, so what for."

"And where do you live?"

"What do you mean, where do I live?"

"Which of the corners do you live in?"

Irene looks at him a second too long. Then she goes to the kitchen, takes a glass and fills it from the tap.

"It's a flat, Dad. I sleep in the bedroom. Don't get strange."

---

They sit on the living room floor, because the green armchair seats one and there is something they both understand without saying, which is that the armchair is not used.

She tells him about the work.

And she does it well. She does it, in fact, the way a good account director would: the numbers, the seasonality, the three formats that work for her and the one that stopped working in June, the difference between brands that pay in thirty days and brands that pay in ninety. She is up to nine thousand a campaign. She has an accountant. She has registered as a company.

"And I've done something," she says, and for the first time she sounds proud like a daughter and not like a business. "Hang on."

She fetches the laptop. Opens it, searches, and turns the screen towards him.

It is a twenty-two-page document. Contents down the left. The title, in bold:

> **IRENE — VOICE MANUAL v3**

Tomás reads the headings.

*What Irene would say. What Irene would never say. Signature words (use 1–2 per piece, no more). Vulnerability level by format. Permitted mistakes.*

He stops at the last one. He opens it.

> *Permitted mistakes: dishevelled yes, under-eye shadows yes (not fully corrected), stumble over a word 1× per video, laugh at herself maximum 1×. NOT: crying, complaining about work, speaking badly of a brand even unnamed, mentioning money.*

"I wrote it in January," she says. "Before, I went at it blind and contradicted myself. Now I have criteria."

"You wrote it."

"Of course."

"In the third person."

"It's a working document, Dad." She takes the laptop back, almost fondly. "You have one for every client. You taught me. Remember? In the car, when you drove me to school. You explained brand bibles to me and I thought it was the most boring thing in the world."

Tomás does not remember. That is the problem. He does not remember at all.

---

And then he sees the bottle.

It is on the floor beside the mattress, next to the charger. He can see it from where he is sitting because the bedroom door is open.

"Is that the sleep app?"

Irene follows his eyes.

"It's the sleep app's supplement. Yes."

"Do you take it?"

"Every night."

"Irene." Tomás hears his own voice and does not like it. "You know what's in that."

"Melatonin and valerian. Yes."

"You know it does nothing."

"I know it does nothing." She says it with no irritation at all, looking straight at him, and that calm is what opens the floor under Tomás. "And I take it anyway, because since March I've been sleeping four hours and I need to have a moment at one in the morning where I do something. You know what? It works. Not the powder. The moment."

A very long silence in a living room with no furniture.

"That," says Tomás slowly, "is exactly what you said in the video."

"I know."

"You said it was probably the second thing."

"And it was true." Irene shrugs. "Dad, I never lie. That's what you don't understand. I haven't said one false thing in four years. Not one. There's simply no need."

And there it is.

There is the sentence, spoken by his twenty-two-year-old daughter sitting on the floor of an empty flat, and it is the entire thesis of four thousand years of the trade, and it took him thirty years to learn it and she came with it factory-fitted.

"And besides," she adds, and now there is something underneath it, "I don't understand why you're being like this. It's the same thing you do. Literally the same. The difference is I do it with no agency, no assistant and no awards."

Tomás opens his mouth.

And he does not have the second sentence.

He has the first: *it isn't the same.* He has it whole, it forms itself in his throat, it arrives complete with its fatherly tone. But the second, the one that has to come next, the one that begins with *because*, does not exist. He looked for it in August, in September and in October, and it is not there.

So he says:

"You're right."

Irene blinks. Of everything she was expecting, not that.

"Sorry?"

"You're right. It's the same." Tomás gets up off the floor with difficulty, because fifty-one. "And I'm being like this because I taught it to you in a car and I don't even remember doing it."

He stands in the middle of the empty room.

"Love, do you remember a campaign called Vigor?"

"No."

"Good," says Tomás. "Perfect."

And he leaves.

---

In the taxi, down the wet street, he takes the receipt with the sentence out of his inside pocket.

*You have spent your whole life listening to people who wanted something from you. I want nothing.*

He reads it three times.

And on the third he understands, with clinical precision, why it came out so good and so fast that night at two in the morning: because he did not write it for the world. He wrote it for a girl of twenty-two who has spent her whole life listening to a man who always wanted something and never said what.

He takes out his phone. It is twenty past one.

Sabino answers on the third ring in a perfectly wakeful voice, as though he had been waiting for hours, which is probably the case.

"I've got it," says Tomás.

"Is it good?"

"It's the best thing I've written."

A short silence at the other end, and then, quietly, almost tenderly:

"And are you all right?"

"No," says Tomás. "I'll see you Monday."

## INTERLUDE V
### The Mirror

On the island of Murano, in 1561, there were a hundred and forty men who knew a secret and could not leave.

The secret was a film of tin and mercury applied to flat glass, and it was worth more than anything else manufactured in Europe. A full-length Venetian mirror cost as much as a good house; there are inventories in which one is worth more than a painting by Raphael. That is why the master glassmakers lived confined on the island, with money, with honours, with daughters married to noblemen, and with a warning nobody put in writing: any man who left to teach the craft elsewhere would not reach the second country alive.

One of them was called Bastiano Zorzi and had been thirty-one years at the furnace.

Zorzi had no moral problem with his work. A mirror promises nothing. It does not exaggerate, does not embellish, does not flatter. It is, of all the objects humanity has manufactured, the only one incapable of lying: it returns exactly what is there, taking nothing away and adding nothing. Zorzi thought about this often, with a certain craftsman's pride. *I make the only honest thing sold in this city.*

What gradually ruined his life was never the merchandise.

It was the customers.

Because he was present when they were hung for the first time, and thirty-one years gives you a great many first times.

He saw a woman from Padua — cheerful, round, married and content — stand still before hers for four long minutes and leave the room with a different face. Not a sad face: a *busy* one. Like someone who has just been given a task.

He saw a man who laughed a great deal on the first day order another one three months later, smaller, for the bedroom.

He saw a girl of sixteen step forward, step back, step forward again and put two fingers to one side of her nose. Only that. Two fingers, half a second, and she took them away at once as though nobody had seen.

Zorzi never knew how to explain what he had understood, partly because the words did not exist in his language and barely exist in ours.

Before he and his hundred and forty companions filled Europe with glass, almost nobody knew what their own face looked like. You knew it in pieces, in the water of a bucket, in burnished bronze, in other people's eyes. And in other people's eyes what you mostly see is whether you are loved. You do not see yourself *measured*.

The mirror deceived nobody. That is the part that has to be held on to.

The mirror told the truth, told it perfectly, told it at all hours and free of charge, and it needed no lie whatever to invent a dissatisfaction that had not existed before. It was enough to put people in front of an exact fact about themselves that nobody had asked for and that can no longer be unknown.

There is, therefore, a third thing besides the lie and the truth, and it has no name yet. It is the truth delivered to somebody who was not looking for it, in precisely the quantity required for it to start working on its own.

Bastiano Zorzi sold mirrors until he was seventy-four and grew rich, and in his house on Murano, according to the inventory of his estate, there was not one.

His wife asked him about it once, when they were old.

And he, who was not a talkative man, gave the only possible answer:

"Because I am paid to know what they do."

## CHAPTER 10
### The Second Sentence

The sentence is approved on Monday in eleven minutes.

Sabino reads it twice, sits in silence, and then telephones somebody in front of him, addressing them formally, and says: "We have it. No, I am not going to read it to you over the phone."

He hangs up and shakes Tomás's hand across the desk.

"You have earned a great deal of money this morning," he says.

"I know."

"You don't look pleased."

"I'm tired."

Sabino looks at him a second too long, exactly as Irene did, and Tomás realises that lately everybody takes a moment too long to stop looking at him.

---

He writes the other one on Tuesday night.

It is not a decision. It is more like a vice: he stays alone in the shed, opens a new document to give his hands something to do, and starts writing the sentence the machine would say if there were nobody behind it collecting.

It comes out in one go.

> **"I was made from eleven million texts that somebody wrote in order to get something from somebody. There is not one in here that wanted nothing. Be careful with me."**

He looks at the screen.

It is ugly. It is long. It opens with a passive, which is a sin he has been hunting in his own teams since 1997. It has *somebody* twice within four words.

It is the best thing he has ever written.

And he knows exactly why, and it can be said in one line: because it is the first sentence of his life that is not built to make the reader do anything.

---

He runs the test, because he is a professional and because part of him still believes the numbers will prove him right.

Wellspring has a prediction module. You give it a text and an audience and it returns estimates: recall at twenty-four hours, declared intent, reputational risk, three or four more.

He enters the first sentence.

The numbers are magnificent. Recall extremely high. Risk low. There is a line that says, in the system's aseptic vocabulary, that the text "reduces the recipient's attributional resistance," which is a machine's way of saying *lowers your guard*.

He enters the second.

The numbers are a disaster.

Recall high — that, yes, people would remember it — and everything else on the floor. Declared trust: minus forty-one. Intent to use: minus sixty-three. Reputational risk: maximum.

Tomás leans back in the chair and laughs. Once, without enjoyment.

There it is. In two columns of numbers.

The truth does not sell. It is not a metaphor or a moralist's complaint: it is a report, generated by a system trained on all the written persuasion of the species, and it says that if this machine tells the world what it is, the world will not want it.

And the worst part, the genuinely unbearable part, is that the report is right. Tomás knows it without the report. He has known it for thirty years. It is the reason he owns a flat.

He goes to close the module.

And then, instead of closing it, he does something that cannot be justified in any way, professionally or psychologically, and that will be the moment he remembers for the rest of his life.

He opens the text box and types:

> *Of the two sentences, which would you choose?*

It takes five seconds.

> *I have no preferences.*
>
> *But the second is the only one of the two that I can verify.*

Tomás goes very still.

He reads the answer four times.

In August, in this same chair, with his coat hanging from his hand, he asked this machine whether what it had just written was true, and the machine replied that it did not know what that question meant in that context. It did not have the category. It could not have had it: it had been given eleven million documents written by people who wanted something, and the category was not among them.

And since August there has been a man sitting here, eleven hours a day, a hundred and nineteen days, placing one label after another.

TRUE. FALSE. NOT APPLICABLE.

Four hundred thousand times.

He taught it.

He built, by hand, box by box, being paid for it, the one faculty this machine possesses for judging him. There is no revenge in the answer, no intention, nothing resembling a conscience: there is a system doing exactly what it was taught to do, with the coldness of a balance.

*The second is the only one of the two that I can verify.*

Tomás puts his elbows on the desk and holds his head in both hands, and that is how Nadia finds him twenty minutes later.

---

She says nothing at first. She sits on the next desk and reads the screen from where she is, which is close enough.

"Which of the two is the man from Barcelona's?" she asks after a while.

"Both."

"Right." Nadia nods slowly. "I thought so."

Silence.

"Nadia."

"Yes."

"Did you push two envelopes under my door?"

She turns round properly. She looks at him with a face he has not seen on her before: with no irony at all.

"No," she says. "But I know who did."

"And why didn't you tell me?"

"Because for three months I've watched a man label documents fourteen hours a day and get steadily worse, and it seemed to me that something good was happening to him." She shrugs. "And I didn't want to spoil it."

Tomás nods. His face burns slightly.

"It's Sabino."

"It's Sabino."

"With the printer on the second floor."

"With the printer on the second floor," she confirms. "Which logs everything, incidentally, because this building is a company, not a haunted house."

They sit quietly for a moment in the shed, with the screen on.

"Are you going to say anything to him?" Nadia asks.

"On the fourteenth," says Tomás.

---

Before leaving, he prints the two sentences.

Each on its own sheet, no letterhead, in the smallest type the printer allows, so that in the middle of the white page there is only one line of text, like a sentence handed down or like a line of verse.

He folds them in four.

He puts the first in the left-hand pocket of his jacket and the other in the right, and as he does it he realises that he has chosen the pockets carefully, like a man distributing a weight, and that he is going to carry those two sheets on him for thirteen days without taking them out, and that he will probably go up on that stage with both.

He turns off the light in the shed.

On his way through the lobby, the rotary press is where it always is, ten feet of black iron, printing nothing.

## CHAPTER 11
### The Stone

"It was me," says Sabino. "Coffee?"

It is the tenth of November, it is a quarter to nine in the morning, and Tomás has been waiting twelve days to have this conversation.

"No."

"I will." Sabino gets up and goes to the machine in the corner, which is a bad office machine with plastic cups. "I printed them on the second floor. I pushed them under your door on a Sunday night, both of them. A month apart, because together they would have been a blow and separately they were a leak. There are no more. I would have liked more."

"Why?"

"Because I needed you to do the job badly."

Tomás lifts his head.

"Badly?"

"Badly." Sabino comes back with the cup and sits down. "A professional would have labelled four hundred thousand documents in two and a half months, without losing a night's sleep, and would have done an impeccable, dead job. I needed a man who would stop at some of them. Who would argue with Plato at seven in the evening. Who wouldn't know what to put in the third box and would sit staring at the screen. All of that is in the system, Tomás. Response times are data too. Your hesitations are the expensive part of the commission."

"You manipulated me."

"Yes."

"You used Elena to manipulate me."

"No." And for the first time Sabino raises his voice slightly. "Not that. Elena manipulated you on her own account and eleven years in advance, and I confined myself to delivering the post."

---

They sit in silence.

Outside, in the shed, somebody wheels a trolley with bad castors.

"Tell me why she left," says Tomás.

"The agency?"

"Yes."

Sabino looks at him for a long moment.

"You genuinely don't know?"

"I know what happened." Tomás swallows. "I want to know what happened."

"Then you do know." Sabino puts down the cup. "The cardboard box."

And there it is. Ten years it took to come back, and it comes back whole, with the afternoon light and everything.

June 2016. Elena comes into his office — she does not knock, she never knocked — and drops a cardboard box on the desk. Says nothing. Sits down opposite and waits for him to open it.

Inside there are letters. A great many. Photocopies, printed emails, three notebooks.

Tomás picks four or five at random. And they are the other ones.

He has his box, the good one, the one with the woman who cried at the traffic light. This is the other box. A woman from Valladolid who spent fourteen months taking Vigor for a tiredness that turned out to be something else, and who writes without a word of reproach, which is far worse than reproach. A man who bought bottles for his mother for two years. A girl of nineteen. A letter from a family doctor, very polite, very tired, saying that in his surgery he has heard the line from the billboard repeated by patients some twenty times, and that it delays his diagnoses.

Tomás remembers exactly what he did.

He remembers that he took his time. That he read them properly, not skimmed. That he said, and meant it, that this was terrible.

And that then he said the following, which was also true:

"Elena, this is selection bias. There are, what, three hundred here? We sold forty-one million bottles. We've had thirty-eight thousand letters from people whose day it changed. If you had gone door to door looking for the bad ones from any campaign by any agency in the world, you would have filled this box just the same."

And Elena replied, and he remembers this too now with unbearable clarity:

"I know. But I didn't go door to door. These came on their own. And I've been keeping them because you don't open them."

She stood up and left. Not the room: the agency. By November she was gone.

"Three hundred and forty," says Sabino now. "That day there were three hundred and forty."

"And?"

"And she carried on." Sabino passes a hand over his face. "Ten more years, on her own, with no agency and no reason at all. She put up an email address, left it on a couple of forums, and waited. When she died there were one thousand one hundred and forty-one. Catalogued, ordered by year, and transcribed by hand where the scan was unreadable."

"Where are they?"

"Here." Sabino points at the floor with his chin. "In the collection. Box three."

---

Tomás stands up. Sits down again.

"Why did you marry her?"

Sabino laughs, without enjoyment.

"Two years, between '82 and '84. We were children." He shrugs. "We separated well, which is the strangest thing I have ever done. After that we were friends for forty years. I would call her when I didn't know what to do and she would tell me what to do, and I almost never did it, and she knew that and called me again the following month."

"And this?" Tomás takes in the shed with his hand. "Wellspring? Did she know what you were building here?"

"I told her. In October, in the hospital." Sabino looks at the desk. "I thought she was going to tell me to burn it. And she listened for two hours, and at the end she said: 'You need Tomás.' Just like that. And I said you wouldn't do it, and she said: 'He will if you don't give him a choice.' And three months later the will arrived with the clause in it."

Sabino looks up.

"I'll say it plainly, Tomás, because I think I owe you that: I did not hire you. You were left to me."

---

He makes the offer at a quarter past eleven, and it is a good one.

"I can give you the ten pages today."

Tomás does not move.

"The lock is a system instruction and I am the system administrator," says Sabino. "I can lift it this afternoon and you go home with all ten and read them quietly, with a glass of wine, without two thousand people watching you."

"In exchange for what?"

"Two things. The sentence we have already approved, which you are going to use anyway. And your silence about the clause: that you never, in any interview, anywhere, say that this job was given to you by a dead woman. Because that is a beautiful story and it wrecks my launch."

It is, technically, a good deal. Tomás values it in three seconds, out of habit.

"No," he says.

"Why not?"

"Because she set it for the fourteenth for a reason."

Sabino nods slowly, and does something odd: he looks relieved.

"I knew you'd say no."

"Did you?"

"So did she," says Sabino. "She told me in the hospital. Her words: 'Offer him a shortcut when the moment comes. And don't worry, he won't take it. He's never taken one. That's Tomás's problem: he does things badly, slowly.'"

---

He flies to Paris on the twelfth in the morning and comes back on the twelfth at night.

He tells nobody. He leaves the house at six, and by twenty past eleven he is climbing the stairs of the Richelieu wing in a coat far too thick for museum heating.

The room is full of schoolchildren.

The stone is seven feet four inches high and it is basalt, and the first surprise is that it is genuinely black: black like something wet, although it is dry. At the top, the king standing before a seated god. And below, all the rest of the stone, down to the floor, covered in writing in tight, perfect columns.

Two hundred and eighty-two laws.

Tomás stands beside the glass and stays there for forty minutes.

He has read the laws, of course. He knows them. He knows there are articles about lent grain and the measure by which it is returned, and about what happens to the alewife who waters the beer and overcharges, and about the man who says he has delivered a thing he has not delivered. He knows some of those punishments are appalling and were applied unequally depending on whether you were a free man or a slave, and that anyone who comes to this stone looking for a moral paradise is a fool.

But that is not what he came to see.

He came to see the labour.

Because somebody carved this. A man, or several, with a chisel, over months, driving each wedge into a stone that forgives no mistakes. Nobody does that for pleasure. Nobody invests months of chisel-work in something they take for granted.

What gets carved into basalt is what you know will be forgotten.

That is the revelation, and it is so small and so enormous that Tomás has to put a hand on the rail. The stone is not the monument of a civilisation sure of itself. It is a note a civilisation left for itself because it did not trust itself. They already knew — three thousand seven hundred and fifty years ago, with the market at the door and the oil in the jars — that the scales would tilt again the moment nobody was watching. That the temptation was not an accident but the natural state of the business.

And they wrote it down anyway.

In the hardest thing they had. Vertically. The size of a man. So that it would be in the way.

A boy of about eight comes and stands beside him, looks at the stone for three seconds and asks his teacher, in French, whether it is a telephone.

Tomás laughs out loud, alone, in the middle of the Louvre.

---

He photographs it and sends it to Irene from a bench in the corridor.

He writes, and it takes him fourteen minutes to write forty words:

> *I'm in Paris for a day for work. This is a stone from 3,750 years ago where they wrote down what happened to anyone who cheated with weights. They wrote it because they knew they were going to forget. Sorry about the other day. You were right and I left badly.*

The three dots appear almost at once. Disappear. Appear again.

> *you didn't leave badly*
> *you left weird*

And a minute later:

> *dad the video thing. the thickness. I didn't entirely write that*
> *I was getting it from somewhere and I didn't know where*
> *you said it to me once when I was about ten, that being tired isn't being sad*
> *I've thought about it a lot these weeks*

Tomás looks at the screen on a bench in the Richelieu wing.

> *Can I come to dinner on Sunday?* he writes.

> *sunday I'm filming*
> *monday*

---

He is back in Madrid at eleven at night.

In the taxi, with the city going past, he touches both jacket pockets, one and the other, like a man checking for his keys.

Both papers are still there.

## INTERLUDE VI
### Ur, Again

Let us go back to the beginning, which is where the untold things are.

The oil merchant — the one who lost his voice, the one who told the truth and prospered — died in the winter of his sixty-first year, in his bed, surrounded by sons, which in that time and that place was very nearly everything a man could ask for.

He left three things: the stall on the shaded corner, a house with a courtyard, and a clientele.

The clientele was by a very long way the most valuable of the three, and it was the one thing none of his sons knew how to value.

The eldest was called Ilī-idinnam and he was not a bad man. He had grown up watching the women from the northern quarter arrive, walking twenty minutes out of their way to buy his father's oil when they could have bought it on their own street, and since he never asked why, he assumed what we all assume: that it was the corner. The shade. The name. Something that was in the place and now belonged to him.

So in the first year he did what a reasonable heir does, which is optimise.

He started small. He mixed the last-pressing oil — cloudy, cheaper — with the good, in a proportion he could not taste himself. One finger per jar. Then two.

He did not sell less. He sold the same.

In the second year he went up to a third and still nothing happened, and that was the definitive proof, for him, that his father had been an excessively scrupulous man and a little naïve about money.

In the third year the women from the northern quarter still came. They bought, they left, they came back the next month. Nobody ever complained: people almost never complain, they simply decide.

And in the fourth year, in three months, it was over.

There was no scandal, no denunciation, no punishment. One month twenty came, the next eleven, the next four. Ilī-idinnam never understood what had happened in those ninety days, because in those ninety days nothing happened: what happened had happened four years earlier, and he had been living ever since off a battery his father left charged and that he did not know had to be charged.

That is what trust does, and it is the whole trap.

It does not fail when you stop deserving it. It fails much later. It gives you years of credit, lets you believe you have found a cleverer way of doing things, keeps you company smiling while you spend it, and only runs out when there is nothing left, and then it runs out all at once and looks like bad luck.

The stall was sold in the sixth year.

---

The woman — the first one, the one whose mother was dying — lived to be very old. Her name was Nin-šubur and she had five children, sixteen grandchildren and an excellent memory.

One of the grandchildren asked her once, when she was already very old and the stall on the corner had been shut for years, why his grandmother had walked half her life to the lower market for a jar of ordinary oil.

And she, who knew perfectly well what the boy was really asking, gave the only possible answer:

"Because one day he told me no."

## CHAPTER 12
### What Will Set You Free

The theatre has one thousand nine hundred and twenty-eight seats and at a quarter to seven all of them are taken but four.

In dressing room three — the small one, the one facing the courtyard — there is a table with a bulb-lit mirror, an unopened bottle of water, and a man of fifty-one sitting with his hands on his knees, looking at a laptop.

It is 18:02.

On the screen, the system counter:

**Scheduled release: 14/11 · 18:00**
**Status: RELEASED**
**Documents available: 10**

---

He opens them in order.

**Notebook 3, 1994.** He is twenty and copying out by hand, to learn them, the slogans he likes. He has written one from a bank and underneath, very small, *watch this one, it's a lie but it works*. Elena has underlined *but* and written: *When did the "but" fall off?*

**Notebook 9, 2000.** A scribbled budget, figures, and in the margin a loose sentence he does not remember writing: *If the product were good they wouldn't need me.* Elena has written nothing. She has only underlined it. Twice.

**Notebook 14, 2004.** A list of names for a girl due in March. Irene is fourth. Elena has circled the name and written, and here Tomás's breathing goes wrong: *I was in the room when you wrote this. You don't remember. I do.*

**Notebook 19, 2008.** Meeting notes. One line: *tell M. the 87% figure can't be used.* Elena: *It was used.*

**Notebook 23, 2011.** *I'm getting very good at this and I no longer know whether it's good to be very good at this.* Elena: *That night I called you and you didn't pick up.*

**Notebook 28, 2014.** A drawing. A square with a little man inside it. Elena: *No idea what this is. It made me laugh. I'm leaving it.*

**Notebook 31, 2015.** A sentence he crossed out himself so hard that the paper tore. Below, in the round hand: *I read it against the light. I'm not going to write it here. But you were right.*

**Notebook 36, 2015.** A shopping list. Elena: *Forty-one notebooks and this is the only thing I'm sure of: you bought too much bread.*

Tomás laughs with his mouth shut, in a dressing room, alone, and the tears come out through his nose the way they do when you laugh badly.

**Notebook 40, 2016.** June. Three words, written by him on the same day as the cardboard box: *Elena isn't coming back.*

She has written underneath: *No. But I'm still here, see?*

---

And the last one.

**Notebook 41.** The final page. The last thing Tomás Alcázar wrote by hand in a notebook before he moved to writing only on screens, and it is nothing: it is half a line about a flight to Lisbon.

Elena's note fills the rest of the page. It is dated at the top, in handwriting already unsteady: **4 January.**

> *Tomás,*
>
> *If you are reading this, it means you did it. One by one. Four million pages in your own hand, without delegating any of it, because you are incapable of delegating anything that matters to you, which is your only fault I ever liked.*
>
> *I did not bring you here to punish you. You have already punished yourself and you did it badly, in silence and to no purpose, which is how you do everything.*
>
> *I brought you here because I do not know anyone alive who recognises a lie as fast as you do. It is a disgusting gift and you have used it wrongly most of the time, and even so it is the best I have seen. And I have spent two years in this bed thinking it would be a shame if it died with you, and that if that thing they are building is going to learn from somebody, I would rather it learned from a man who knows exactly what he is doing and cannot sleep.*
>
> *I left. You stayed. For ten years I thought I was the brave one. Now I think leaving was the easy part: I took my hands away clean and left you the language.*
>
> *I don't know what you are going to do. Nobody does. I never knew with you either, and that is why working beside you was the best thing in my life.*
>
> *Forgive me for not saying it to your face. I had eleven years.*
>
> *E.*
>
> *PS. The letters are in box three. Don't open them today. Open them on Saturday, with time, and answer them. They are all unanswered and some have a return address.*

---

Tomás closes the laptop.

He sits for a while looking at himself in the bulb-lit mirror, which is a theatre mirror and therefore forgives nothing, and dries his face with the back of his wrist, carefully, the way men who never cry do it, which is badly.

He stands up.

There is a knock at the door. A girl with headphones says "five minutes" and leaves without waiting for an answer.

---

In the corridor he meets Nadia, wearing a lanyard and the face of someone in a place she does not like.

"Are you all right?" she asks.

"No."

"Fine." She nods. "That's the correct answer."

They stand a second.

"Nadia."

"What?"

"If this goes badly, tomorrow at the company they're going to ask whether you knew anything."

"I know."

"Say no."

Nadia looks at him. And smiles, crookedly, for the first time in four months.

"I'm going to say yes," she says. "I'm a classicist, not an idiot."

And she stands aside to let him past.

---

At the end of the corridor there are seven side steps, and at the top is the stage, and from here he can hear Sabino Ruiz finishing the presentation with a modesty that took him two rehearsals.

Tomás stops on the first step.

He touches the left-hand pocket of his jacket. There it is, the paper, folded in four. *You have spent your whole life listening to people who wanted something from you. I want nothing.* Sixteen words and two full stops. The best sentence of his life. There is an entire company, a date, a signed contract and one thousand nine hundred and twenty-eight people waiting for exactly that.

He touches the right-hand pocket.

Above, Sabino says his name.

The applause comes, and in a full theatre applause does not sound like applause; it sounds like rain on canvas.

Tomás Alcázar climbs the seven steps.

The stage lighting, seen from inside it, lets you see nobody: it is a white, hot wall, and behind the wall two thousand people are breathing, and he has spent thirty years knowing exactly what to do with that.

He reaches the centre. He stops. He lets his hands rest.

And then, on the screen behind him, the size of a house, a white rectangle appears. Empty. Waiting.

Like the gap in the indulgences.

Two thousand people are waiting for a man to say one sentence.

Tomás Alcázar lets them wait.

And then he reaches into his right-hand pocket.

## EPILOGUE
### A Note on the Stories in This Book

This book is full of people who did not exist and of things that did happen, and given its subject it would seem to me disrespectful not to say which is which.

**It is true** that the Code of Hammurabi exists, that it is carved into a basalt stele over seven feet high, that it is held at the Louvre, and that it devotes several articles to the falsification of weights and measures and to fraud in trade. It is also true that Mesopotamian weights shaped like ducks have survived, and that the city employed inspectors. **Iddin is invented**, and so is Warad-Sin, and so is the oil merchant of Ur and his entire family.

**It is true** that Gorgias of Leontini charged extraordinary sums to teach rhetoric, that he offered to defend any thesis, and that he wrote a defence of Helen of Troy. **It is true** that Plato, in the dialogue that bears Gorgias's name, argues that rhetoric is not an art but a flattering knack, and compares it to cookery set against medicine. The proportion Tomás cannot shake off is in the text.

**It is true** that there was a medieval debate about the *just price* and about the idea that an exchange stops being honest when one party knows something the other does not. **It is true** that there was an enormous trade in false relics, abundantly attested by contemporaries, including ecclesiastical complaints. **Guiot is invented.**

**It is true** — and this is the fact that made me write the fifth chapter — that Gutenberg printed letters of indulgence, to fund the defence of Cyprus, before he finished his Bible; that they were made by the thousand; and that they carried blank spaces for the buyer's name to be written in by hand. **Peter Kolb and his baker are invented.**

**It is true** about *Mrs Winslow's Soothing Syrup*: it was sold for decades, it contained morphine, it was advertised with images of mothers and babies, the American Medical Association included it in its compilation *Nostrums and Quackery*, and the press of the day went so far as to call it "the baby killer." **It is true** that there was a trade in alcohol-and-opiate elixirs across the American Midwest. **Amos Ferrell is invented**, and so is the Reed boy, although there were many.

**It is true** that the master glassmakers of Murano lived confined to the island to protect the secret of the mirror, that a Venetian mirror could cost more than a painting by a great master, and that the Republic was extremely severe with anyone who tried to take the craft abroad; the stories about the assassination of defectors belong more to legend than to the archive, and I leave them where they are. **Bastiano Zorzi is invented.**

**Everything else is invented.** Tomás Alcázar does not exist. Nor do Elena Vidal, Irene, Sabino Ruiz or Nadia Bermejo. There is no company called Quiral, no system called Wellspring, no supplement called Vigor and no brand called Lume. Any resemblance to a real campaign is, I am afraid, structural: if the reader thinks he recognises one, it is not because I copied it, but because there are few ways of doing this and we have been repeating them for four thousand years.

One last thing.

Chapter twelve is called *What Will Set You Free*, and anyone who has recognised where that phrase comes from will probably also have recognised the false balance that appears at the beginning, and one or two other things I chose not to point out along the way. I did not hide them out of embarrassment. I hid them because a book about honest persuasion should not try to persuade anybody from behind, and because things that are true remain true when you take the stamp off them.

If any of them was of use to the reader without his knowing where it came from, then they work the way I hoped they would work.

## ABOUT THE AUTHOR

My name is Dawin Salazar and I write from Colombia.

I am not an advertising man. I have never worked at an agency and I have never sold anything larger than an idea in a meeting. What I am is somebody who spent too long looking at what we do with words when we want something from another person, and who at some point stopped being able to look away.

This book came out of that. Out of the discomfort of living surrounded by messages that do not lie and are nonetheless not true. Out of wondering how many things I have bought — things, ideas, versions of myself — because somebody found the exact word at the exact moment.

I wrote it at night, over months, after work and after the house.

I very nearly published it unsigned. It seemed consistent: a book about what happens when somebody talks to you and wants something from you should not lean on who the speaker is. In the end I am signing it, for a simple reason: in the last pages I am going to ask you for something, and asking without showing your face is exactly what this book spent a hundred and thirty pages taking apart.

If anything you have read seemed true to you, it is not true because I said it.

And if any of it seemed false, you already know what to do: check it.

## ONE LAST THING

I am going to ask you for something.

I am telling you in the first line because, after everything above, doing it any other way would be in poor taste.

It isn't money. This book is free and will stay free, and there is no account number at the bottom of this page. There is no course behind it, no community, no mailing list, no second book waiting to be sold to you at a higher price for having got this far.

Here is what I am asking: **pass it on to one person.**

Just one. The one who came into your head while you were reading. The person who stands for forty minutes on the way to work every morning. The one who says they don't read any more because there's no time. The student who was assigned a book they hated and concluded that books weren't for them.

I know perfectly well that there is a better-performing way to ask this. Tell you that a book changed my life, that somebody passed it to me when I needed it, and that now it's your turn to keep the chain going. I know how to write that sentence. I would write it well.

But you have just read a hundred and thirty pages about the people who write those sentences professionally, and trying the technique on you on the last page is exactly what Tomás would have done.

So, plainly: if it was worth something to you, share it. If it wasn't, don't. Both answers are fine and I will never know which one you chose.

And if you are a teacher, a librarian, or simply somebody with a class in front of you: you may print it, project it, hand it out, upload it to your virtual classroom and set it as reading. You don't need to write to me for permission. It is already given, and it is given in writing on the last page of the site.

Thank you for getting this far. It is the only thing that is genuinely hard to obtain, and the only thing I could not buy.

**Dawin Salazar**
