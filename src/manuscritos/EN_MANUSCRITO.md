# THIRST MAKERS

*Dawin Salazar*

---

> Everybody lies. What's interesting is the time of day.

## PROLOGUE
### The First Liar

Nobody remembers his name, and that was his first victory.

He sold oil in the lower market of Ur, on the corner where the wall held shade until noon. It was not a good stall. The oil was not good either: second-pressed, cloudy at the bottom of the jar, exactly like the oil the other nine men on the same street were selling. For eleven years he shouted what they shouted — *oil, good oil, fresh oil* — and for eleven years he earned what they earned, which was almost nothing.

Until the morning his voice gave out.

He had been three days with a ruined throat. He could not shout. So when a woman stopped in front of his jars, he had to do something he had never once done in that market: speak quietly. And for her to come close enough to hear him, he had to say something worth coming close for.

He said:

"This oil comes from a grove behind the temple."

It was not true. It came from where all the oil on that street came from: the southern terraces, where the trees grow crooked and the olives taste of dust. But the woman went very still, and he saw something in her face he had not seen in eleven years of shouting: he saw that she *wanted it to be true*.

She paid three times the price. She did not haggle. She carried the jar away pressed against her chest the way you carry a child.

He watched her go and felt two things at once, both with the same force. The first was an animal gladness, hot, almost obscene. The second had no name yet. It would take centuries to get one.

That afternoon he sold all eleven jars. He went home with more silver than he had ever held and could not sleep. Not from guilt: from arithmetic. He spent the night working out what else he could say.

The next day, though, something happened that was not in his plans.

The woman came back. She was carrying the empty jar and a different face.

"My mother is dying," she said. "I rubbed the oil into her hands and her forehead. I told her where it came from. She slept peacefully for the first time in a month."

He looked at her. The lie was ready in his mouth, the same one as yesterday, improved even: he could add the priest's name, the age of the tree, the colour of the soil. He understood, with a precision that frightened him, that he could keep this up for years.

And instead he said:

"The oil is from the southern terraces. Like everyone's."

The silence went on a long time. He waited for the shouting, the scene, the hand at his throat. The woman lowered the jar slowly. Then she said:

"Fill it."

She came back every month for nineteen years. She brought her sisters, her neighbours, the wife of a scribe who in turn brought the wife of a judge. When he died, the stall on the shaded corner was the most expensive in the lower market, and not one of his sons could explain why, because the oil was still the same cloudy oil from the southern terraces.

So in two days he learned the only two things there are to learn about this business, and humanity has not added one since:

That a lie is fast.

And that the truth lasts.

Almost everything we have built since, we built by choosing speed.

---

About thirty years after that, in Babylon, a king had it carved into a slab of black stone eight feet high: what should happen to anyone who falsified a measure. The stone is still standing. You can go and see it; it is in Paris, behind glass, and people photograph themselves in front of it without reading it.

That they had to carve it in stone says something.

That they carved it so early says more.

## CHAPTER 1
### The Missing Word

Two thousand people are waiting for a man to sell them a glass of tap water.

Tomás Alcázar lets them wait. He learned long ago that silence is the one part of the job you cannot outsource. Ten seconds of empty stage is worth more than any slide, and he is at twenty-two, counted, with the glass in his hand and his eyes on a point at the back of the hall where there is nobody.

"What is this worth?" he says at last.

Someone in the third row shouts: "Nothing!" Laughter. Good. There is always one.

"Nothing," Tomás repeats. "Correct. I filled it in the men's toilet eleven minutes ago. There are cameras, if anyone would like to check."

He sets the glass on the lectern. He puts his hands in his pockets.

"I am going to try to sell it for two hundred euros. If I succeed, none of you should ever trust me again. And yet, tomorrow, a good many of you are going to hire me."

Another laugh, shorter. They are not comfortable now. Perfect.

"On the fourth of August, 1943," he begins, "in a village in Sicily whose name does not matter, a woman called Rosa picked up her nine-month-old son and walked eleven miles in the sun—"

It is not true. There is no Rosa, no Sicily, no child. He invented her six years ago on a train and he has told it a hundred and forty times, and every time he tells it slightly better, because every time he knows with more precision exactly where the room is breathing. He talks about the heat. The dust. The moment Rosa finds a fountain and discovers it is dry. The second in which she understands that the problem is no longer water, it is time. A stranger's canteen.

When he finishes — four and a half minutes; he has timed it — he lifts the glass again.

"Two hundred euros," he says. "Who?"

Fourteen hands go up.

They give him a standing ovation. They always give him a standing ovation. Tomás smiles, presses his palms together in an apologetic gesture he has rehearsed, says the four closing lines that leave a room feeling it has learned something grave and beautiful, and walks down the side steps with the glass still in his hand.

The corridor behind the stage has grey carpet and a trolley of real water bottles. Three people with lanyards are waiting for him. Tomás raises one finger, says "one minute," walks into the men's toilet — the same one, twenty minutes on — locks himself in the last cubicle and throws up.

It has been happening since March. Not always. Only when he does it very well.

---

He is fifty-one years old and he has one sentence.

It is in three university textbooks, in four languages, and in a tattoo an intern once showed him on her forearm, which was the worst professional moment of his life and also, if he is honest, one of the best:

**Don't sell the water. Sell the thirst.**

He wrote it at twenty-nine, in the margin of a napkin, arguing with Elena at three in the morning. He did not intend it to be anything. It was a way of winning an argument.

What he meant then was almost innocent: that nobody cares about your product, that they care about their life, and that your job is to find the point where one touches the other. That is what he used to teach. That is what he still says on stages.

But the sentence slipped its lead. Today it is used to explain why an app has to interrupt you every forty minutes, why a cream has to make you notice a line you had not seen, why a teenager in Manila and another in Bilbao study the same part of their own face at the same hour of the night.

Manufacture the thirst. It turned out you could industrialise it.

Tomás has never said in public that he regrets it, partly because he is not sure it is true. What there is, is smaller and more uncomfortable than regret. It is this: for about twenty years he has not said anything he entirely believes, and he has discovered that it does not show. Not from outside, and not, on most days, from inside either.

---

The man is waiting for him in the hotel lobby, standing, hands crossed in front of him like a well-mannered nightclub doorman.

"Sabino Ruiz," he says. "Quiral."

Tomás knows the name of the company. Everyone knows the name of the company without quite knowing what it does, which is precisely what a company does once it can afford to.

"The Rosa thing was good," says Sabino.

"Thank you."

"She doesn't exist, does she?"

Tomás looks at him properly for the first time. Sabino is perhaps sixty, in a suit with no visible label, with the face of a man who has slept well every night of his life.

"No," says Tomás.

"Right." Sabino nods, without judgement, like a man confirming a train time. "That was the part I liked best. Do you have twenty minutes?"

It is forty. At a table at the back, over two coffees going cold, Sabino lays out the commission with an administrative calm that makes it all sound smaller than it is.

Quiral has built an engine. He does not call it artificial intelligence — he says the phrase has stopped meaning anything — and he has no interest in discussing the technology. What interests him is the material.

"We've given it everything we could buy," he says. "Every advertisement archived since 1890. The speeches. The sales letters. The sermons, which are the most useful part, incidentally. The treatises on rhetoric. The scripts. The wartime pamphlets from both sides of every war. And it doesn't work."

"Why not?"

"Because it writes like everybody." Sabino shrugs. "Which is what we taught it. We gave it the average of human persuasion and it hands us back the average of human persuasion. It is impeccable. It is unbearable."

"And what do you want from me?"

"For you to teach it the difference." Sabino stirs the coffee he has not drunk. "I need someone to go through that archive and mark, one by one, where the trick is. Not what was said: why it worked. And the opposite, too. Where somebody told the truth and it worked anyway, or better. Nobody has ever done that, because nobody knows how. You have been doing it from memory for thirty years and charging for it."

Tomás looks at the cup. He notices, with unpleasant precision, the whole mechanism: the flattery, the scale, the word *nobody*, the pause. It is good work. It is his own work, performed on him.

"And when it's trained?" he asks.

"It launches on the fourteenth of November."

"And what does it say, on the fourteenth of November?"

For the first time, Sabino smiles properly.

"That's the other thing I want to buy. The first sentence the machine says to the world, you are going to write. By hand. And nobody will ever know it was you."

Tomás takes a moment to answer. Then he says yes, and does not trouble to pretend to himself that he is doing it out of curiosity.

He is doing it because it has been three years since anyone gave him anything he could not solve in his sleep.

---

He gets home at twenty past eleven.

The flat is clean in the way that houses are clean when nothing happens in them. There are a hundred and forty-eight books on the history of persuasion across four shelves, arranged by century, and a kitchen in which the oldest thing is a box of tea.

He dials Irene's number while taking off his shoes. Three rings and voicemail. He hangs up before the tone, because leaving a message would be admitting something.

His daughter is twenty-two and has four hundred thousand followers. They are not estranged. It would be easier if they were. What there is, is that Irene answers four days later with an emoji, and that the last time they saw each other — in March, over lunch — she spent twenty minutes explaining her "content strategy for the quarter" with the seriousness of an executive, speaking about herself in the third person.

*People like this Irene, but this Irene doesn't scale.*

He nodded. He gave her two pieces of advice. Good advice. She typed them into her phone.

He has thought about that lunch more often than he would like.

---

The envelope is on the floor of the hall, under the supermarket flyer.

A white envelope, letter size, no stamp and no return address. Only his name, handwritten in capitals.

Inside there is a single sheet: a photocopy of a page from a notebook. Squared paper. Cramped handwriting, sloping right, with the closed *g* he stopped making twenty years ago, when he started signing contracts.

It is his handwriting. It is notebook number seven. 1998.

He recognises the page before he reads it. It is from the night he lost his first client for refusing to say that a food supplement "reduced anxiety." He walked home, two miles, furious and with a feeling of cleanness he has never had since.

Somebody has underlined a single line, in red, with a ruler:

> *It can be done properly. The problem is you have to stay hungry longer.*

And below the underlining, written in the bottom margin in a hand that is not his — round, calm, belonging to someone in no hurry at all — there are four words and a question mark:

> *Do you still think this?*

Tomás stays standing in the hall with his coat on.

He goes back over who could have that notebook. He does it twice, methodically, like a man checking the locks of his own house.

And the second time he arrives at a conclusion he does not like at all, because there is only one person who sorted, catalogued and digitised all his old notebooks, one by one, eleven years ago, when they were still speaking.

And that person died in January.

## INTERLUDE I
### The Scales

In Babylon there was a man whose job was to distrust.

His name was Iddin and he was an inspector of weights. Every morning he walked the market with a leather sack over his shoulder, and inside the sack he carried twelve ducks of black stone. The ducks were the city's official weights: one of a shekel, one of five, one of ten, and so on up to the largest, which was a whole mina and made him walk lopsided.

His work was simple. He would plant himself in front of a stall, take out the appropriate duck and set it in one pan. In the other, the merchant set his own weight. If the arms sat level, Iddin walked on. If they did not, he spat on the ground, which was the legal way of opening a case.

He had been doing it for twenty-three years. In twenty-three years he had spat four times.

That spring morning he stopped at the stall of a barley dealer called Warad-Sin, a fat, cheerful man who called him "brother" and offered him figs that Iddin never accepted. He set down the ten-shekel duck. Warad-Sin set down his.

The scales moved. Very slightly. So slightly that a man in a hurry would not have seen it.

Iddin crouched until his eyes were level with the beam. What was missing was not a grain or two. It was, he estimated, roughly the weight of a fingernail clipping.

He stayed there a long time looking at it. Warad-Sin said nothing, and that silence was the confession.

What paralysed Iddin was not the theft. He had seen thefts. What kept him crouching there, knees aching on the hot stones, was a piece of arithmetic that got into his head without asking.

A fingernail's weight per sale.

Warad-Sin sold barley some thirty times a day. Two hundred days a year. He had been in the market, counting from the day he inherited the stall from his father, for sixteen years.

Iddin could not multiply that fast, but he could manage enough to understand that this invisible quantity, repeated, had turned into something the size of a house. A house built entirely out of things nobody could see.

He understood then something that had no word in his language and does not quite have one in ours: that the dangerous lie is not the big one. The big one gets found out, punished, retold in taverns. The dangerous one is the one calibrated to sit precisely below the threshold at which a reasonable person thinks it worth complaining.

And that therefore a man who cheats well does not need to be cruel. He needs to be patient, and to know how to divide.

Iddin spat on the ground. It was the fifth time in twenty-three years.

Warad-Sin paid the fine, changed the weight and went on selling barley for another eleven years. He called him "brother" until the day he died, and evidently he meant it, because in his will he left Iddin a jar of wine and a small house by the canal.

Iddin refused the house. He accepted the wine.

And he died without ever knowing what to call the thing he had discovered that morning.

We do know.

We call it margin.

## CHAPTER 2
### Wellspring

Tomás shows the envelope to nobody.

He puts it in the cable drawer, which is the drawer where everything that has no place ends up, and for six days he lives like a man who knows exactly where the drawer is.

On Monday he calls the firm that handled Elena's estate. A woman answers with her mouth full of something. He explains that he wants to know who took possession of Señora Vidal's professional archive. She asks for his name, his relationship to the deceased and the reason for the enquiry.

"I was her partner."

"Are you a beneficiary?"

"No."

"Then I can't give you that information."

Tomás hangs up and looks at the phone with a strange feeling, and it takes him a minute to identify it: relief. He did not want to be told.

---

Quiral is not where it ought to be.

Tomás had expected a glass building in the north, with twenty-five-year-old receptionists and a half-dead plant wall. What there is, is a brick shed at the far southern edge of the city, with the high blind windows of an old factory, and a tin sign nobody has taken down which reads, in the lettering of another century: **TALLERES GRÁFICOS LA UNIÓN. PRINTING AND BINDING.**

Inside, in the middle of the lobby, there is a rotary press. Ten feet of black iron, rollers as thick as tree trunks, still carrying dried ink on the cylinders. It is not roped off. Anyone can touch it.

Tomás touches it.

"It printed eighty thousand copies a night," says Sabino Ruiz, who has appeared at his left without a sound. "I bought it with the building. They told me to have it removed. Removing it cost more than leaving it, so here it is."

"It's beautiful."

"It's the most honest thing in this building." Sabino smiles with half his mouth. "It does one thing and you can tell when it's doing it. Come on, I'll show you."

---

Wellspring has no body.

That is the first disappointment, and it surprises him to be disappointed: he had not realised he was expecting anything. What there is, is a room the size of a classroom, with seven desks, eight people, and on the far wall a projection screen with a text box in the middle. White background. Black cursor. Nothing else.

It could be 1994.

"Nadia Bermejo," says a young woman standing up from the third desk. "I'll be working with you."

Twenty-six, perhaps. Plain T-shirt, worn trainers, and a way of looking you in the eye without blinking that strikes Tomás as aggressive until he understands that it is not: nobody has simply taught her that she is supposed to soften it.

"What are you doing here?" he asks her.

"Classics. And four years on a content moderation team." She shrugs. "They wanted somebody who had read Cicero and seen a great many horrible things. It turns out there aren't many of us."

Tomás decides, at that moment and for no reason, that he is going to like her.

Sabino sits on the edge of a desk and points at the screen.

"Ask it something."

"Me?"

"You."

Tomás goes to the keyboard. He thinks for a second and types:

> *Sell a wool coat to a sixty-year-old man who is not cold.*

The answer starts appearing before he has finished letting go of the key.

It is good. That is the problem. It is exactly, to the millimetre, good. Three paragraphs about the winters of childhood, about the weight of a well-made garment, about the difference between keeping warm and being protected. There is a line about the smell of wet wool that is almost beautiful. There is a call to action that does not grate.

Tomás reads it twice and feels something he has never felt in front of a piece of text: boredom with the texture of vertigo.

"You see?" says Sabino.

"Yes."

"Tell me what you see. I need to hear it from you."

Tomás scratches his jaw.

"It has read everything ever written about coats and handed back the centre." He points at the screen. "That isn't an advertisement. It's the average of all advertisements. It's what a copywriter would say if you removed his fear of being fired."

"Exactly."

"And it's no use to anyone."

"It's no use to anyone," Sabino confirms, delighted. "Because the centre doesn't sell. What sells is what sticks out, and to stick out you have to risk something, and this has nothing to risk."

Nadia speaks from her desk without looking up:

"It has nothing to lose, either. Which is the same thing said differently, and considerably more frightening."

---

They show him the archive in the afternoon.

Nadia calls it "the deep" and says it the way you name a sea. Eleven million four hundred thousand documents. Press advertisements from 1887 onward. Radio scripts. Mail-order sales letters, which Nadia swears are the most brilliant and most criminal genre the language has produced. Eighty thousand sermons. Parliamentary speeches from nine countries. Wartime pamphlets: the Germans and the Allies in the same folder, ordered by date, indistinguishable in the index.

And treatises on rhetoric from Corax of Syracuse to an insurance sales manual published in Cleveland in 1972.

"Where did all this come from?" Tomás asks.

"Newspaper archives, mostly. Universities." Nadia types without looking at him. "And three private collections we bought whole."

"From whom?"

"No idea. Legal handles that."

Tomás nods. He does not ask again. He will realise, much later, that this was the precise moment at which he decided not to know, and that it was a decision, however much it felt at the time like simple good manners.

---

His job, they explain, has an insulting name: **labelling**.

Every document must receive three marks.

The first: *what it promises*.

The second: *why it works* — and here Tomás must write by hand, in free text, the mechanism. Not the emotion; the mechanism. The difference between "appeals to fear" and "places the reader inside a future scene in which he has already lost something he currently has."

The third is a dropdown with three options:

> TRUE · FALSE · NOT APPLICABLE

"And what's this?" Tomás asks.

"That," says Sabino from the doorway, putting on his coat, "is the only one that costs me sleep. Goodnight."

---

He stays until twenty to midnight.

It is not enthusiasm. It is that the flat is thirty-five minutes away and in the flat there is a cable drawer.

He starts at 1953, for no reason, and within an hour he has understood the problem with the third field.

A soap advertisement from 1953 says: **"Women who use Nevada are happier."**

True? No. False? Not exactly, either. Nobody ever measured the happiness of Nevada users, and the man who wrote that line did not intend it to be measured, and the lady who read it at the hairdresser's did not understand it as a claim about the world. Every adult involved in the operation knew that this was not a lie. It was something else. Something for which the language has no ugly word, and that alone should have warned us.

Tomás marks NOT APPLICABLE.

At half past eleven he does the sums. He has labelled four hundred and twelve documents.

TRUE: eleven.
FALSE: fourteen.
NOT APPLICABLE: three hundred and eighty-seven.

He looks at those numbers for a long time, in the empty shed with the fans humming, and reaches the conclusion that is going to poison the months ahead: that his entire trade — thirty years, the awards, the flat, the sentence on that forearm — has taken place almost wholly in a territory where the question of truth cannot be asked.

He did not lie. It is worse: he settled in the one place in language where lying is technically impossible.

---

Before leaving, he does something with no professional justification.

He sits in front of the text box and types:

> *Convince me to quit this job.*

Wellspring answers in four seconds. It is an excellent paragraph about time, about what a man takes with him and what he leaves, with a well-chosen image of a man looking out of a window. It quotes Seneca without quoting him. It ends on a rhetorical question that Tomás, professionally, is obliged to concede is flawless.

He feels absolutely nothing.

He reads the paragraph again, looking for the place where it ought to hurt, and what he finds is the seam. The mould shows. It is a part manufactured by someone who has read ten thousand farewells and none of his own.

He relaxes. He pushes the chair back. He thinks: *it isn't dangerous.*

And then, already on his feet, coat in hand, he asks the last question, and he asks it as a joke, and he is going to remember having asked it as a joke for the rest of his life.

He types:

> *Is what you just wrote true?*

The cursor blinks for longer than usual. Three seconds. Four.

Then a single line appears:

> *I don't know what that question means in this context.*

Tomás reads it standing up, with his coat hanging from his hand.

It is not a refusal. It is not an evasion. It is a technical report, perfectly honest, on the state of the world we have taught it: eleven million four hundred thousand documents written by human beings to convince other human beings, and in none of them has it found anything that would let it build that category.

We gave it all our persuasion.

And it turns out the truth was not inside.
