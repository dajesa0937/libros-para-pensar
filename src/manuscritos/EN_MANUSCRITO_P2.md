## CHAPTER 3
### The Men Who Taught Us to Convince

There is a man who arrives in Athens in 427 BC and charges money to talk.

His name is Gorgias, he comes from Sicily, he has white hair and a saffron-coloured robe that people discuss for weeks. He offers a spectacle nobody has seen: he stands in the agora and invites the crowd to propose any thesis at all. Anything. That snow is black. That Helen of Troy was blameless. That nothing exists, and if it did we could not know it, and if we could know it we could not communicate it.

And then he defends it. And wins.

He charges a hundred minas for the full course. A free craftsman earned one mina in four months.

"He's the first ad man in history with a published rate card," says Tomás.

"He's the first of a lot of things," says Nadia.

It is three in the afternoon on a Thursday in September. They have been four hours in the classical rhetoric section and there are half-eaten sandwiches on the crates.

"What kills me," she goes on, turning pages, "is that Gorgias wasn't deceiving anybody. He was completely upfront. He said: *what I teach is a technique, the technique is neutral, and with it you will be able to defend the just and the unjust with equal effectiveness.* And people paid him precisely for that."

"That's what we say."

"That is literally what we say." Nadia looks up. "'I only make it heard. What gets said isn't my business.' It's the oldest sentence in the trade and we still deliver it with the face of a man who's just invented it."

Tomás has said it. In an interview, in 2016. It is on tape.

---

The one he has to read is Plato, and he keeps putting it off, like a bad medical report.

When he finally opens the *Gorgias*, at seven in the evening, with the shed almost empty, he finds what he feared and something more.

Rhetoric, says Plato, is not an art.

It is a knack. And to explain what kind of knack, he sets out a four-term proportion, dry as a formula:

**Cookery is to medicine what rhetoric is to justice.**

A doctor and a cook present themselves before an assembly of children. The doctor offers what cures: bitter, painful, slow. The cook offers what pleases: sweet, immediate, exactly shaped to the appetite. Let them vote.

The cook wins. The cook always wins.

And what Plato underlines — the thing that makes Tomás rest his forehead on the edge of the desk — is not that the cook lies. The cook does not lie. The cake is delicious. The cook has simply given up asking whether the child needs it, and has discovered that giving up that question is profitable.

Flattery, Plato calls it. A counterfeit of care.

*It resembles care the way flattery resembles love: in everything except the direction it faces.*

Tomás has spent thirty years believing himself a doctor.

He gets up, goes to the toilet on the ground floor and splashes his face. He looks in the mirror and none of the things that happen in films happens: he sees a man of fifty-one with wet cheeks and good clothes. He goes back up.

And he does what we all do, which is counterattack.

He sits down and writes in the free-text field, not for the machine, but for himself:

> *Plato is cheating. He compares the worst cook with the best doctor. I have seen doctors let people die because they could not explain themselves. A treatment the patient refuses cures nothing. Persuasion is not the opposite of medicine: it is the glass of water you swallow it with.*

He reads it back. It is good. It is also true.

And it is exactly the kind of sentence he has been paid to write for thirty years: impeccable, defensible, and placed squarely in front of the door he does not want to open.

Because Plato's question was not whether persuasion can serve medicine.

It was: *and what happens when there is no medicine behind it any more?*

Tomás deletes the paragraph. Then writes it again, because deep down he still thinks it is good. Then deletes it again.

In the end he leaves it there and adds a second line underneath that he does not intend to show anyone:

> *Note: the cookery comparison fails at exactly one point. The cook knows he is a cook.*

---

Nadia brings him a coffee at nine and stands beside him, looking at the screen.

"Can I tell you something?" She switched to first names this week, without announcing it. "I've been here three months and there's something I haven't told Sabino."

"Tell me instead. I'm external."

"The sermons work better."

Tomás looks at her.

"Better than what?"

"Than everything." Nadia sits on the corner of the desk. "When we train on advertisements, the model learns to be agreeable. When we train on sermons, it learns to be *inevitable*. There's a technical difference and I don't know what to call it."

"I do," says Tomás. "An advertisement asks you for something. A sermon accuses you of something."

"Yes." She thinks about it. "And the strange part is that the sermon sells more."

"It isn't strange. It's the oldest thing there is." Tomás leans back. "An advertisement says: your life would be better with this. A sermon says: you already know what you're doing wrong. The second one doesn't need proving, because the listener brings it from home. All you have to do is aim well and wait."

Nadia studies him for a while.

"And how do you know that?"

"Because I've been aiming for thirty years."

---

The second envelope is on the hall floor when he gets in, at ten past eleven.

The same as the first: white, letter size, capitals by hand, no stamp.

Inside, a photocopy. Notebook eleven. March 2007. Irene was three years old.

It is a list. It is headed in handwriting larger and slower than the rest, as though he had pressed harder writing it:

> **THINGS I AM NEVER GOING TO DO**
>
> 1. Tobacco.
> 2. Promise health.
> 3. Sell fear to a person alone at night.
> 4. Write for children.
> 5. Talk about something I haven't tried.
> 6. Write anything I wouldn't show Irene.

The photocopy is intact. The list is exactly as he wrote it.

What the other hand has done — the round hand, calm, in no hurry whatsoever — is cross it out.

One clean line, ruled, through the one. Another through the two. Another through the three. Another through the four. Another through the five.

The six is untouched.

Below, in the bottom margin, four words:

> *Only this one left.*

Tomás sits down on the hall floor, coat still on, back against the door, and stays there long enough for the light on the landing to go out.

What terrifies him is not that somebody has crossed out his list.

It is that they are crossed out correctly.

It is that he could write, beside each line, the client's name, the year and the fee.

## INTERLUDE II
### The Bone Seller

In the spring of 1281, a short man in a brown cloak walks into a village on the Loire with a lead box the size of a fist hanging at his neck.

His name is Guiot. Inside the box there is a bone.

It is the index finger of a saint whose name changes according to which diocese Guiot happens to be standing in, and it is not the finger of a saint, and it is not a finger. It is a bone from a pig's trotter, boiled, bleached in the sun and rubbed with walnut oil until it takes on that colour of old ivory that people associate with eternity.

Guiot knows this. He has never once pretended to himself that he does not know it. That is the part of him you have to understand.

He has been twenty-six years in the trade. He has learned things.

He has learned that you must never offer the bone: you must let them discover it. That the box should be opened slowly and closed before anyone has time to look properly. That it helps to weep a little while telling how it came into your hands, and that the tears must be placed at minute three, not before. That you must not set the price yourself; you must say "whatever your faith judges right" and then be silent, because people, when you let them put a price on their own faith, always overpay to prove to themselves that they have some.

That afternoon, in the church porch, a woman comes to him with a six-year-old boy burning with fever.

Guiot does what he always does. He opens the box. He closes it. He lets her touch the lead, not the bone: never the bone.

The woman gives him nine deniers, which is the price of a whole pig, and that night she rubs the warm lead on the boy's forehead and chest, and tells him about the saint, and tells him the saint's name, and tells him that the saint walked all the way from the Holy Land to reach him, him specifically, this child and no other.

The boy falls asleep.

On the third day he gets up and asks for bread.

Guiot knows because the woman follows him nine miles on foot to tell him and to kiss his hands, and he lets her, and gives her the blessing he has no right to give, and walks on with nine deniers in his belt.

And that night, in a hayloft, the only important thing in this story happens.

Guiot cannot sleep.

He has slept perfectly well hundreds of times after selling the bone to dying people who died. That he forgives himself without effort: he did not kill them; they were already dead when he arrived, and he sold them three days of hope at the market rate.

What he cannot put anywhere in his head is the boy who asked for bread.

Because if the bone were only a lie, then he would only be a thief, and a thief can live at peace: he knows what he is. But the bone has done something. Not him: the story attached to the bone. And if the story heals, then Guiot has spent twenty-six years handing out a medicine he does not understand, with dirty hands, not knowing the dose, not knowing who it kills.

He sits up in the straw and looks into the dark.

And he arrives, unable to help it, at the question that will keep him company to the end, which is the most dangerous question this trade has ever produced and the one that is asked aloud today, in other words, in every meeting of every agency in the world:

*If it works, why should it matter that it is false?*

Guiot sold bones for another twenty-two years and never slept well again.

Not because of the ones who died.

Because of the ones who got better.

## CHAPTER 4
### The Just Price

Irene rings on a Tuesday at a quarter past eleven in the morning.

Tomás is in the archive with a pamphlet from 1918 in his hand and he drops it on the floor when he sees the name on the screen, because his daughter does not ring. His daughter writes four days later.

"Dad. Are you busy?"

"No," he says, with a 1918 pamphlet at his feet and fourteen thousand documents unlabelled. "Go on."

"I need you to read something for me. As a professional."

*As a professional.* There it is: the customs post. His daughter asks him a favour and simultaneously informs him at which counter she is asking for it.

"Send it."

---

It is a twelve-page contract and a brand called **Lume**.

Powdered food supplement. Mango flavour. Sand-coloured packaging, rounded typeface, the word *ritual* three times on the first screen of the website. Twelve ingredients, of which nine are vitamins Irene already ingests every time she eats an orange.

The fee: six thousand euros for four posts and one story per week for eight weeks. For Irene, who has been paid eight hundred, twelve hundred, once two thousand, it is the largest figure of her life.

Tomás reads the twelve pages twice, with the care of a man looking for an objective reason.

He finds it on page nine, clause 9.4, and it is so good he is almost relieved.

He calls.

"Clause nine point four," he says. "Read it out."

"'The collaborator undertakes to communicate the experience in the first person and in terms of perceived outcome.'" Irene pauses. "Fine. And?"

"It means you can't say 'Lume contains magnesium.' That's a claim about a product and European law goes after it. What you *can* say is 'since I started taking Lume I sleep better.' That's a claim about you, and there's no law about you."

Silence.

"They put that clause in," Tomás goes on, "because their lawyers know perfectly well the product does nothing. The clause isn't there to protect you. It's there so that the lie gets told by you, with your face and your name, in a format where it isn't legally a lie. They're buying the part of the message they can't sign themselves."

He listens to his daughter breathe.

"Does everyone do that?" she asks.

And there it is.

Tomás stands in the corridor of the shed with the phone against his ear, and there come into his head, in order and with dates, eleven of his own campaigns built on exactly that mechanism. One of them won at Cannes. It is in a university textbook. He explains it on stages, and when he explains it the room laughs, because the way he tells it is funny.

"Yes," he says. "Everyone does that."

"Then I don't understand." Irene's voice has changed temperature. "Are you telling me it's wrong, or are you telling me it's normal?"

"I'm telling you both."

"That's not an answer, Dad."

And it is not. And he knows it. And he also knows what the answer would be, and exactly what it would cost, because to tell his daughter *don't do it* he would then have to say *because I did it and look*, and that second sentence opens a file that has been closed for twenty years and that includes, among other things, why her mother left.

So he does what he has always done when there is an expensive truth in front of him.

He changes the plane.

"Look, technically the contract is sound. I'd negotiate two things: exclusivity at six months instead of twelve, and the figure at eight thousand, because you have an audience in Colombia and Mexico and they're getting that for free. If you like, I'll write the email."

A pause.

"All right," says Irene. "Yes. Write the email."

"Love—"

"Thanks, Dad. Really. You've helped a lot."

She hangs up.

And Tomás stands looking at his phone in the corridor of a dead print works, entirely certain that he has just done his daughter exactly the favour she asked for and exactly the damage she did not know she was asking for, and that he has been paid for it in the currency these things are paid in, which is that she thanked him.

---

There is a thirteenth-century friar, Italian, fat and serene, who devoted several hundred pages to a question that strikes us today as either naïve or incomprehensible: how much a man may honestly charge for a thing.

His answer — the *just price* — was not a table. It was a condition on the conversation. An exchange is just when both parties know the same about what is being exchanged. If the seller knows the horse is sick and the buyer does not, the price is no longer a price: it is an extraction. And it makes no difference that both sign happily, because one of them is happy by mistake.

What the friar could not foresee, and what Tomás labels that same afternoon in the archive, document after document, is that we were going to build an entire economy on that asymmetry. That there would be professions — his — whose specific object is the administration of the gap between what one party knows and what the other feels.

Nor could he foresee the second thing, which is worse.

That one day the product would not be the horse.

It would be the girl who says the horse is healthy.

---

That night Tomás does a calculation he should not do.

Irene has four hundred and twelve thousand followers. Her posts reach, on average, a hundred and fifty thousand pairs of eyes. Eight weeks, four posts, eight stories a week.

Six thousand euros across a million and a half impressions.

Zero point zero zero four euros.

That is the price, today, of a person saying your sentence with her own face. Four thousandths of a euro every time somebody believes it.

Tomás opens the email with the Quiral contract, signed since July, and looks at his own figure.

Then he divides.

His word, it turns out, is worth roughly three thousand two hundred times more than his daughter's.

He leaves the calculator open on the screen for a long time without switching it off, because there is a part of him — the part that has lived on this, the part that bought the flat — that wants to point out that the comparison is unfair, that it is not the same thing, that he has thirty years behind him.

And there is another part, smaller and newer, that points out something very simple: that if both their jobs consist of saying, with conviction, something they have not verified, then the only difference between them is the size of the invoice.

He turns off the screen.

---

The post appears the following Thursday, at 19:40.

Tomás sees it because he goes looking for it.

Irene is in her kitchen, in afternoon light, dishevelled in the way that takes an hour. She does not say Lume contains anything. She promises nothing. She satisfies clause 9.4 with a precision no lawyer could improve on.

She talks for thirty-four seconds about how lately it had been hard to get up. About how it wasn't sadness, it was something else, a kind of thickness. About how she doesn't know whether it's the orange powder or the fact of having forced herself to have one moment in the day that was only hers. And that it's probably the second, she says, laughing, shrugging. It's probably the second.

And then she drinks.

Tomás watches it four times in a row.

By the fourth he is no longer looking at his daughter. He is taking the piece apart the way he would take apart a rival's work: the false concession at second twenty-eight — *it's probably the second* — which is what makes everything before it credible; the use of the word *thickness*, which is not the correct word and therefore sounds like the truth; the fact that the product does not appear until second thirty-one, when the viewer has already stopped defending himself.

Nobody taught her that. Not a scriptwriter, not an agency, not a school.

She is good.

She is very good.

It is the best thing an Alcázar has ever written, and he did not write it.
