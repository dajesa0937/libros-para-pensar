## CHAPTER 5
### The Multiplying Machine

What everybody knows about Johannes Gutenberg is that he printed the Bible.

What Tomás finds on a Wednesday in October, at twenty past four, in box 3,114 of the archive, is that before the Bible he printed something else.

It is a small sheet, a hand's breadth by two. Thirty-one lines of cramped gothic type. And in the middle of the sheet, cutting through the paragraph, there is a rectangle of blank paper.

A gap.

Tomás looks at that gap for a long time before he reads the catalogue card.

It is a letter of indulgence. Year 1454. They were sold to fund the defence of Cyprus against the Turks: you paid, and in exchange a quantity of time was struck off your stay in purgatory. The blank space is for your name. The seller wrote it in by hand, in front of you, with your own pen if necessary.

Thousands were printed.

Before the forty-two-line Bible, before Luther, before modern science and journalism and the novel, the first thing Europe mass-produced with the new machine was a promise about life after death, sold for cash, with a space reserved for personalisation.

Tomás gets up and walks to the window.

There is a technical term for that. He uses it every day. It is the name of a function in every email program in the world.

*Mail merge.*

Fourteen fifty-four.

---

"That's what I've been saying since I got here," says Nadia, without emotion, when he goes down to tell her. "The history of persuasion has no revolutions of content. Only revolutions of flow."

"Explain."

"That we never invented a new argument." She pushes the keyboard away. "Look: take a preacher in the year one thousand. He can convince three hundred people on a Sunday, if he shouts. Gutenberg doesn't teach him to speak better; he prints him three thousand sheets. Radio doesn't invent emotion; it puts it in twelve million kitchens at the same hour. Television doesn't discover desire; it puts it in colour."

"And us."

"And us." Nadia points at the blank screen at the far end of the room. "We haven't invented anything either. What we do is a different sermon per person, per second, for ever. Same bullet. What goes up is the rate of fire."

Tomás leans against the desk.

"There's a problem with what you're saying."

"Go on."

"That if it's the same bullet, then there's no moment when this went wrong." He notices he is speaking more slowly than usual. "I've spent four months looking for the point. The date. The year advertising stopped informing and started doing the other thing. And it doesn't turn up."

"It doesn't turn up because it doesn't exist," says Nadia. "The only thing that has happened in four thousand years is that we kept driving down the price of repetition."

---

Sabino Ruiz comes into the room at seven, as he does three or four times a week, for no apparent reason and always with his coat on, as though he were passing through his own company.

Tomás shows him the indulgence.

Sabino looks at it for thirty seconds. Then he does something that disarms Tomás: he laughs. A short laugh, sincere, almost affectionate.

"Oh, that's lovely," he says. "The gap. They knew about the gap."

"You find it lovely?"

"I find it honest." Sabino puts the sheet down. "Look at what they didn't do. They didn't print the name. They could have run one print run per parish. They left the gap deliberately, because they already knew that a document with your name written on it by another person's hand binds you in a way a printed one does not. That's fine knowledge, Tomás. That's the work of people who had looked a great many buyers in the eye."

"It's a swindle about purgatory."

"Yes." Sabino shrugs. "And?"

Tomás says nothing.

"I'm asking seriously, not to provoke you." Sabino sits down at last, on the edge of the desk, and loosens his scarf. "You want me to say that was the original sin and everything went crooked from there. I'm telling you there was no original sin. I'm telling you that a man in Mainz with a machine that cost as much as a house had to print something that would sell itself, and indulgences sold themselves because they answered a fear that existed before the machine. He didn't manufacture the fear of hell."

"No. He only distributed it."

"Exactly." Sabino points a finger at him, pleased. "And that is the whole profession, mine and yours, and it has been the same since before it had a name. We don't manufacture the thirst. We find it and put a tap beside it. That the tap is there is a service. That it costs money is a business. And the only thing that has changed is that we can now put the tap in front of each person at the exact moment they are thirsty."

"And if the tap gives no water?"

Sabino looks at him. For the first time since they met, he takes a while to answer.

"Then that's a different conversation," he says at last, "and it isn't an engineering conversation. It's a moral one, and there is nobody in this building hired to have it."

He stands, rearranges his scarf, and adds from the doorway without turning round:

"Which is why I hired you, incidentally. Whatever the invoice says."

---

Irene writes to him that night. Four messages in a row, which has not happened for two years.

> *dad the lume thing went well*
> *really well*
> *three other brands have written*
> *one's a sleep app and they pay triple*

Tomás reads the four messages standing in the kitchen.

He types: *Congratulations, love.* He deletes it.

He types: *Irene, we need to talk about something.* He looks at it for thirty seconds and deletes that too, because he knows perfectly well that if she answers *about what?*, he does not have the second sentence.

In the end he sends an emoji.

He stands there with the phone in his hand in the dark kitchen, and thinks, with a clarity that is no use to him at all, that a man who does not have the second sentence does not have an opinion: he has an irritation.

---

Before going to bed he does something.

He opens the laptop and writes to Nadia, although it is twenty past midnight.

> *Strange question. Can you see where the three private collections in the archive came from? Not the papers: the acquisition metadata. Who sold, when, and on what conditions.*

She takes four minutes to reply. One message.

> *I can. Why?*

Tomás looks at the cable drawer from the far side of the corridor.

> *I don't know yet.*

## INTERLUDE III
### Ink

Mainz, 1490. A journeyman printer called Peter Kolb discovers what ink does.

He does not discover it by reading. He discovers it out of boredom, which is how almost all dangerous things are discovered.

He has been nine years pulling the lever. Nine years feeding in sheets, pulling out sheets, hanging them to dry like laundry. He has printed psalms, municipal ordinances, calendars, a fish price list, and four thousand eight hundred sheets about a comet that was going to end the world and ended nothing.

And one afternoon in November, while setting a sheet of town notices, a piece of mischief occurs to him.

He adds a line nobody commissioned.

It is short. It says that the bread from the bakery on Bridge Street has sawdust mixed into the flour.

It is not true. Peter has nothing against the baker on Bridge Street; he does not even know him. He does it for the same reason a boy throws a stone into a pond: to see the ring.

He prints two hundred sheets. He hangs them to dry. The next day they are distributed around the town.

And then he starts watching.

The first thing that surprises him is that nobody investigates. Nobody goes to the bakery to ask. Nobody demands proof. The second is that the news does not travel the way spoken rumours travel, with the *they say that* stuck to the front of it like a warning. It travels clean. Naked. People do not say *I read that the baker puts sawdust in.* They say *the baker puts sawdust in.*

And the third, which is what will cost him his sleep: after three weeks, somebody tells it to him.

A man in the tavern, one who cannot read, tells him with details Peter never printed. That the sawdust is pine. That he has done it since his wife died. That his brother-in-law brings it from the sawmill in sacks at night.

Peter listens to his own lie come back at him, fattened by people who improved it for free.

In February, the baker on Bridge Street closes. In April he leaves the town with his two daughters.

Nobody ever comes looking for Peter Kolb, because it occurs to nobody that somebody wrote that. That is precisely the point, and it takes him a few more years to be able to say it in words.

A spoken lie has a mouth. You can look at the mouth. You can see whether the man's hands shake, whether he looks away, whether he has been drinking, whether he owes you money, whether he hates the baker. The lie arrives wrapped in the person telling it, and you have spent your whole life — your whole species has spent its whole life — learning to read that wrapping.

What printing does is not to convince better.

What it does is remove the mouth.

And a sentence with no mouth does not look like anybody's opinion. It looks like the state of the world.

Peter Kolb went on printing for another twenty-six years and never again added a line of his own. He taught the trade to two apprentices, and told both the same thing on the first day, and both thought it was an old man's nonsense:

"There's nobody in here. Remember that when you read."

## CHAPTER 6
### Everything That Cures

The box is labelled **PATENT MEDICINE — NORTH AMERICA — 1860/1930** and contains, according to the card, forty-one thousand two hundred documents.

It takes Tomás three days to understand that he has arrived at the centre of his trade.

Not because it is the worst. Because it is the most beautiful.

The advertisements for the miracle remedies of the nineteenth century are, by a distance, the best commercial prose ever written. The men who wrote them had no charts, no photography, no data, no branding: they had the language and an absolute desperation, and with that they built a genre. There are paragraphs in there that Tomás could not match, and he knows it.

And then there is the other thing.

**Mrs Winslow's Soothing Syrup** was sold for seventy years in eleven countries. The illustration is a mother bending over a cradle. The text is about relief, about the night finally quiet, about the swollen gum of a baby who is at last at rest. It is tender. It is well written. It does not promise to cure anything: it promises calm.

And it contained morphine.

Tomás sits with the cursor hovering over the dropdown with the three marks.

Because the baby did calm down.

The advertisement told the truth. The syrup did exactly what the advertisement promised. The mother bought it, gave it, the child stopped crying, the house went quiet, and the advertisement was confirmed by experience. And in 1911 the American Medical Association included it in a book whose title Tomás copies out in full in the free-text field, because he does not want to forget it:

*Nostrums and Quackery.*

And the press of the day called it, without ornament, *the baby killer*.

Mark: **TRUE**.

It is the first time in four months that a label frightens him.

---

He writes it that night, for nobody, in the notebook he has started buying again:

> *The advertisements that have done the most damage in history did not lie. They told the truth about the effect and kept quiet about the mechanism.*
>
> *"The baby sleeps" is true.*
> *"The baby sleeps because you are giving him morphine" is also true.*
> *The whole business fits between those two sentences.*

He reads it back and adds one line underneath, and his hand shakes slightly as he writes it, which annoys him considerably:

> *Vigor.*

---

**Vigor** came onto the market in October 2001 and changed everything.

It was a bottle of two hundred capsules: magnesium, valerian, B vitamins and an extract of a Peruvian root that sounded ancient. It did nothing. Not that it did little: it did nothing that a reasonable diet did not do for free, and Tomás knew it, because within thirty days of taking the account he had read the four existing studies and all four were paid for by the manufacturer.

Elena came into his office with the folders and dropped them on the desk.

"It's water that tastes of soil," she said. "Tell them no."

"We've already told them yes."

"Then untell them."

They were thirty-two and thirty-four, with eight employees and a debt that kept them both awake, separately, at the same hour.

The rest of the argument, twenty-five years on, Tomás does not remember in full. He remembers two things.

He remembers that he won.

And he remembers how.

Because he did not win with an economic argument. He won because *the missing word* came to him, and it came to him right there, in front of her, and the moment he said it aloud they both knew the argument was over.

The entire sector was selling energy. Photographs of people running at dawn, men taking stairs two at a time, the word *performance* everywhere. They were all talking about the future: *you will be capable of more.*

And Tomás realised, watching a woman asleep on the underground with her mouth open, that you cannot promise more to exhausted people. To exhausted people the word *more* is a weight.

What you have to do with exhausted people is absolve them.

The campaign showed nobody running. It showed a woman sitting on the edge of a bed at a quarter to seven in the morning, doing nothing, her feet on the floor. And above her, in small type, the only sentence Tomás Alcázar has written in his life that people learned by heart:

**You are not tired. You have been strong for a very long time.**

Elena read the line, closed her eyes and said:

"You bastard."

And they signed.

---

And here is what Tomás has never been able to explain to anyone — not in interviews, not on stages, not to Elena when she walked out fifteen years later.

The sentence is true.

It is not an exaggeration. It is not an unkeepable promise. It is not a claim about a product. It is a true thing, and a delicate one, and a necessary one, and there are people — he received the letters, he keeps them in a box — who wrote to tell him they burst into tears at a traffic light on reading it on a billboard, because they had spent eleven years not allowing themselves to be tired and an advertising hoarding gave them permission.

They sold forty-one million bottles.

Of water that tastes of soil.

That is the trade. It does not consist of telling lies. It consists of finding a truth so exact and so needed that the person, grateful, picks up whatever you have placed beside the truth and takes it home.

Mrs Winslow's Soothing Syrup promised calm and delivered calm.

Vigor promised absolution and delivered absolution.

In both cases the bottle was the least of it, and in both cases the bottle was the entire business.

---

At eleven at night, alone in the shed, Tomás does something he has been avoiding for a week.

He opens Irene's video again with the sound off, and reads the automatic subtitles as though they were a document from the archive.

> *it wasn't sadness. it was something else. a kind of thickness.*

And there it is, twenty-five years apart, without his daughter ever having seen the campaign, because in 2001 she had not been born:

You are not tired. You have been strong for a very long time.

It isn't sadness. It's a kind of thickness.

The same machine. The same exact movement: first you take away from the person the ugly name she had given herself, you absolve her, and into the warm space the absolution leaves, you place the bottle.

His daughter has reinvented his campaign without knowing it existed.

Tomás closes the laptop and sits in the dark, and for the first time in the whole book he thinks the word *inherited*, and he thinks it in the sense in which a disease is inherited.

## CHAPTER 7
### Elena's Archive

Nadia takes six days and tells him on the fire escape, smoking, which is where these things get told.

"Three collections," she says. "The first belonged to an advertising man from Barcelona who died in 2019; they bought it from the heirs, two hundred thousand pieces, mostly sixties press. The second is a university holding from Illinois — that's where nearly all the patent medicine box comes from."

"And the third?"

Nadia draws on the cigarette.

"The third is called, in the system, 'Collection V'. Forty-three thousand documents. Date of acquisition: the sixth of February this year."

Elena died on the ninth of January.

"Donation, not purchase," Nadia goes on. "Zero euros. Executed by a probate firm in fulfilment of the donor's last will."

"Vidal."

"Elena Vidal Aguirre." Nadia glances sideways at him. "Who, from what I could read on the internet on the underground this morning, founded an agency with you that was named after the two of you."

Tomás nods. His throat feels wrong.

"And what's in Collection V?"

"Her entire professional archive. Forty years." Nadia stubs the cigarette out against the railing and puts the butt in her pocket, which strikes Tomás, at that moment, as moving. "And forty-one scanned notebooks that aren't hers."

---

He goes down alone.

He sits at the workstation at the back, the one facing the wall, and types his own surname into the search box.

The forty-one notebooks come up. One to forty-one. Scanned at three hundred dots per inch, in colour, with the folded corners visible, the coffee stains, the phone numbers written in the margins belonging to people who no longer exist.

Date of digitisation: between March and September 2015.

Eleven years before she died. One year before she left.

Tomás imagines it. He can see it perfectly: Elena in her house in Guecho, with the scanner somebody gave her, going through forty-one of his notebooks page by page for six months, without telling him. Four thousand-odd pages of the handwriting of a man she still shared an office with and no longer spoke to.

That is not archiving.

That is reading someone.

---

It takes him another twenty minutes to dare to run the search he came to run.

He filters by annotations. The system has a field for it: it flags scans containing added ink that does not belong to the original document.

The counter returns a number.

**12**

Twelve pages, out of four thousand one hundred and sixty, carry a red line ruled with a straight edge and a question written in the margin in a round hand.

Tomás opens them in order.

The first is from notebook seven. *It can be done properly. The problem is you have to stay hungry longer. Do you still think this?* It arrived in his letterbox in August.

The second is the crossed-out list. It arrived in September.

And the other ten are there. All of them. Right now. One click away.

Tomás puts his hand on the mouse and does not move it.

He realises, with unpleasant lucidity, that if he opens them tonight something ends: he will stop receiving them and start having read them, which is not the same. And he also realises that what he has just thought is exactly the sort of elegant reasoning he manufactures when he is frightened.

He opens the third.

And he cannot.

**ACCESS RESTRICTED BY INSTRUCTION OF THE DONOR.**

The same with the fourth. The fifth. All ten.

And below, in grey, a system line:

**Scheduled release: 14/11.**

Tomás sits looking at that date on a screen in a dead print works, at half past nine on a Thursday evening in October.

The fourteenth of November.

The day Wellspring launches.

The day the whole world will hear, for the first time, a sentence he wrote.

---

Sabino is still in his office. Tomás walks in without knocking.

"She attached a condition," he says. "To giving you the archive."

Sabino closes the laptop slowly. He does not look surprised and he does not look guilty. He looks like a man who is finally being asked the right question after a long wait.

"Two conditions," he says. "The first is that the collection could not be resold or broken up."

"And the second is me."

"The second is you."

Tomás sits down because his legs ask him to.

"Verbatim," says Sabino, "because I learned it: *the labelling of the collection shall be carried out, in its entirety, by Don Tomás Alcázar Ruano. Should he refuse, the donation is revoked.*"

"And were you going to tell me?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because in August you were a charming man who sold tap water on a stage and went home happy." Sabino folds his hands. "And I needed somebody to do this job well, and this job is only done well if it hurts. She knew that. So did I. We have both done the same thing to you, Tomás, and she did it first and with better handwriting."

Tomás looks at the desk.

"Did you know her?"

And for the first time since the hotel, Sabino Ruiz lowers his eyes.

"Forty years," he says. "We were married for two."

## INTERLUDE IV
### The Pedlar

The boy's name was Tobias Reed, he was four years old, and he died in Coffeyville, Kansas, on the third of August 1892, at three in the morning.

The man who sold his mother the bottle was called Amos Ferrell and did not find out until the Thursday.

Ferrell sold *Dr Marsh's Kickapoo Vegetable Elixir*, which was made by no Dr Marsh and had nothing vegetable about it: it was thirty per cent alcohol, water, burnt sugar, oil of turpentine and laudanum. It was good for coughs, for colic, for the nerves, for rheumatism and for whatever else was needed, and in a sense it was good for everything, because laudanum makes everything matter less.

He found out on the Thursday because the boy's father hunted him through three towns and found him unhitching his horse behind the Methodist church in Independence.

Ferrell heard the man out entirely. Twelve minutes. He did not defend himself once, which the father had not expected and which disarmed him more than shouting would have. At the end he gave back the dollar twenty with his hat in his hand and said he would pray, and it was not a lie: he prayed that night and several after.

And then, in the wagon, by lamplight, he did the only important thing in this story.

He denied nothing.

Amos Ferrell was not stupid and he was not a monster, and both things have to be held at once or none of this makes sense. What he did was far more effective than denial, and it is an operation that any person of any century will recognise the moment they see it written down:

He divided.

He told himself it had been the fever, because the boy already had a fever when she bought the bottle.

He told himself that if it had been the elixir, it had been the dose, and he puts the dose right there on the label, in large letters, one teaspoon.

He told himself that if it had been the dose, the dose had been given by the mother, at eleven at night, with a child screaming, in the dark, with a soup spoon because she could not find the other one.

He told himself that if it had been him, then what about the four hundred-odd bottles he had sold that summer to people who got better and came back for more, some of whom had walked miles to come back for more, and he had the letters.

And he told himself, last, the sentence that always closes this series and that sounds so reasonable you have to say it aloud a couple of times to hear what is underneath it:

*If I don't sell it, somebody else will, and he'll do it worse.*

Not one of those sentences is false. That is the point. Each one, taken alone, is defensible in a courtroom, and in fact won two.

What happens is that between the five of them there is not one gram of responsibility left anywhere. It has been cut into pieces so small that they no longer weigh anything. And the man who climbs down from the wagon in the morning is neither a murderer nor an innocent: he is a professional with a bad memory, and you can carry that around for a whole lifetime without it showing from outside.

Amos Ferrell sold elixir for another eleven years.

In his account book for August 1892, which is held at a university in Illinois and digitised under reference number 8,331,907, the summary of the week of the Reed boy reads, in cramped and correctly spelled handwriting:

> *Sales: 31. Returns: 1.*
